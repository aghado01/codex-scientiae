# Abstract

Latent space models are widely used in statistical network analysis and are often fit by Markov chain Monte Carlo. However, posterior summaries of latent coordinates are not canonical because the likelihood depends only on pairwise distances and is invariant under rigid motions of the latent space. Standard post hoc alignment can aid visualization, but the resulting summaries depend on an arbitrary reference configuration. We propose a quotient-based posterior analysis for Euclidean latent space models using the centered Gram map, which represents identifiable latent structure while removing nonidentifiability. This yields intrinsic posterior summaries of mean structure and uncertainty that can be computed directly from posterior samples, together with basic theoretical guarantees including canonicality, existence, and stability. Through simulations and analyses of the Florentine marriage network and a statisticians’ coauthorship network, the proposed framework clarifies when alignmentbased summaries are stable, when they become reference-sensitive, and which nodes or relationships are weakly identified. These results show how coherent posterior analysis can reveal latent relational structure beyond a single embedding.

Keywords: Bayesian network analysis, latent space model, nonidentifiability, posterior summary, quotient geometry

# 1 Introduction

Latent space models (LSMs) provide a flexible and interpretable framework for statistical network analysis (Hoff et al.; 2002). Let G = ( V,E ) be an undirected graph with node set V = { 1 ,...,n } and observed adjacency matrix

$$
A = ( A _ { i j } ) \in \{ 0 , 1 \} ^ { n \times n } , \quad A _ { i j } = A _ { j i } , \ A _ { i i } = 0 ,
$$

where A ij = 1 indicates an edge between nodes i and j . In the standard Euclidean LSM, each node i has an unobserved latent position x i ∈ R r , collected as X = ( x 1 ,..., x n ) ⊤ ∈ R n × r . Conditional on X , edges are modeled as conditionally independent with

$$
\mathbb { P } ( A _ { i j } = 1 \, | \, X , \alpha ) = g ( \alpha - \| x _ { i } - x _ { j } \| ) \, , \quad 1 \leq i < j \leq n ,
$$

where g ( · ) is an inverse link function and α is an intercept. Since their introduction by Hoff et al. (2002), Euclidean LSMs have been studied largely in Bayesian form, with priors on ( X ,α ) and posterior inference based on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). A typical specification is x i iid ∼ N ( 0 ,σ 2 I r ) and α ∼ N (0 ,τ 2 ), although our development does not depend on a particular prior. Throughout, we use LSM to mean Euclidean LSM.

In practice, posterior draws are only intermediate objects. Scientific conclusions are conveyed through posterior means, credible regions, uncertainty displays, and predictive summaries. Such summaries are meaningful only if they target identifiable structure. In LSMs, however, the likelihood depends on X only through pairwise distances and is therefore invariant under rigid motions of the latent space. Under common isotropic priors, the posterior typically retains rotational and reflectional invariance. Consequently, coordinatedependent summaries such as posterior means of x i or coordinate-wise credible intervals are not canonical as they may change under arbitrary reorientation of the latent space even when the posterior is tightly concentrated in relational terms.

A common practical response is to center posterior draws and align them to a reference configuration via orthogonal Procrustes matching (Hoff et al.; 2002; Gower and Dijksterhuis; 2004). This is useful for visualization, but it is unsatisfactory as an inferential primitive because the resulting summaries depend on an external reference, such as the first draw or a point estimate, that is not part of the Bayesian model.

We address this problem by carrying out posterior analysis on a representation that encodes only identifiable latent structure. Specifically, we map each latent configuration X to its centered Gram matrix Φ( X ) := HXX ⊤ H , where H is the centering matrix, where the map is invariant under rigid motions and preserves all pairwise distances. Pushing the posterior through Φ yields an induced posterior distribution on a quotient state space of centered, low-rank positive semidefinite matrices. Posterior summaries are then defined directly on this quotient space, and hence depend only on the induced posterior distribution of identifiable structure, not an arbitrarily specified reference outside of the model.

A natural question is why translation is removed as well, given that common Gaussian priors break translation invariance of the posterior. The point is that our inferential target is not the absolute origin of the latent coordinate system, which is prior-dependent, but the relational latent structure encoded by pairwise distances. Centering chooses a canonical representative of each distance-equivalent configuration while preserving all pairwise distances exactly. In this sense, quotienting out translation is a choice of estimand rather than a claim that translation remains a posterior symmetry.

This quotient perspective naturally prioritizes rigid-motion-invariant inferential quantities and builds on classical distance geometry and multidimensional scaling, where Gram matrices provide rigid-motion invariants of point configurations (Gower and Ross; 1969; Borg and Groenen; 1997). Geometrically, it also aligns with quotient-manifold treatments of fixed-rank positive semidefinite matrices under orthogonal group actions (Absil et al.; 2008; Journ´ ee et al.; 2010; Massart and Absil; 2020), while our use of geometric summaries draws on statistics on manifolds (Bhattacharya and Bhattacharya; 2012; Patrangenaru and Ellingson; 2016; Pennec et al.; 2020). Conceptually, the issue is analogous to label switching in mixture models, where meaningful posterior summaries must be invariant to permutations rather than tied to arbitrary labels (Celeux et al.; 2000; Stephens; 2000; Jasra et al.; 2005).

This paper makes four contributions.

- 1. We formalize a quotient state space for LSMs based on centered Gram matrices, capturing identifiable latent structure while removing rigid-motion nonidentifiability.
- 2. We define intrinsic posterior summaries on this space, including a Fre´chet posterior mean, global dispersion, intrinsic credible regions, dyad-level summaries for distances and edge probabilities, and node-level uncertainty indices.
- 3. We provide practical algorithms to compute these summaries directly from posterior draws, so the framework can be used as a post-processing layer on top of existing MCMC routines for LSMs.
- 4. We establish basic theoretical properties, including canonicality, existence of Fr´echet means on a closed state space, stability under perturbations of the posterior law, and consistency of distance-based inference under posterior concentration.


The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the identifiability issues underlying LSMs and clarifies why alignment-based summaries are not suitable inferential primitives. Section 3 develops the centered Gram representation and the quotient geometry used throughout. Section 4 introduces intrinsic posterior summaries and compu- tational procedures, while Section 5 establishes their basic theoretical properties. Section 6 reports a simulation study together with analyses of the Florentine marriage network and a statisticians' coauthorship network, illustrating both the instability of reference-based summaries and the interpretability of quotient-based uncertainty. Section 7 concludes with a discussion of implications and extensions.

# 2 Motivation and Background

We begin with notation. We write ∥ · ∥ F for the Frobenius norm and ∥ · ∥ for the ℓ 2 norm. The orthogonal group is O ( r ) = { R ∈ R r × r : RR ⊤ = I r } , where I r is the r × r identity matrix. The centering matrix is

$$
H = I - \frac { 1 } { n } 1 _ { n } 1 _ { n } ^ { \top } ,
$$

where 1 n is the lengthn vector of ones, so that H 1 n = 0 and H ⊤ = H . For n ∈ N , we write [ n ] := { 1 ,...,n } .

# 2.1 Identifiability in latent space models

In LSMs of the form (1), the likelihood depends on X only through pairwise distances. Thus, for any orthogonal matrix R ∈ O ( r ) and translation vector t ∈ R r ,

$$
\| ( R ^ { \top } x _ { i } + t ) - ( R ^ { \top } x _ { j } + t ) \| = \| x _ { i } - x _ { j } \| ,
$$

so the likelihood is invariant under the rigid-motion group action

$$
g \cdot X \coloneqq X R + 1 _ { n } t ^ { \top } , \quad g = ( R , t ) \in O ( r ) \times \mathbb { R } ^ { r } .
$$

This invariance is structural, directly rooted in using inter-point distances in the model.

In its Bayesian form, LSMs typically employ priors that concentrate latent positions near the origin, most often independent isotropic Gaussians. Such priors break translation invariance of the posterior by penalizing configurations far from the origin. Consequently, translation is not a posterior symmetry even though it remains a likelihood symmetry. This distinction helps to clarify why centering posterior draws is coherent and widely used, rather than an arbitrary post hoc convention.

By contrast, isotropic priors do not break rotational or reflectional invariance. Both likelihood and prior remain invariant under the map X  → XR for R ∈ O ( r ), so the posterior assigns equal probability to all rotated and/or reflected versions of a configuration. This property makes the orientation as a fundamentally unidentifiable object.

# 2.2 Posterior sampling versus posterior summarization

Rotational non-identifiability does not pose a problem for MCMC sampling. The chain may explore equivalent orientations, and rotational variability in the draws is rather a faithful representation of posterior uncertainty under the model. Difficulties arise only when one attempts to summarize the posterior. Many common summaries are defined in the ambient Euclidean coordinate system, e.g., E [ x i | A ] or coordinate-wise credible intervals. However, the posterior distribution is not naturally a distribution on R n × r with identifiable coordinates. Instead, it is a distribution on equivalence classes under orthogonal transformations. As a consequence, coordinate-dependent summaries are not uniquely determined by the posterior distribution alone.

Consider a simple thought experiment to illustrate this point. Suppose the posterior concentrates sharply near a single configuration X ⋆ up to rotation, so that draws look like X ( m ) ≈ X ⋆ R m with R m varying roughly uniformly in O ( r ). Then the coordinate-wise posterior mean would be approximately zero by symmetry, even though the latent structure is highly concentrated. This example asserts that the issue is not uncertainty. Indeed, it shows that even the simplest posterior summary measure such as the mean is taken in an inappropriate non-identifiable coordinate system, which leads to an unreliable conclusion.

# 2.3 Limitations of alignment-based summaries

As noted earlier, a widely used remedy is to align posterior draws using orthogonal Procrustes matching. After centering, each draw is rotated to match a chosen reference configuration, and posterior summaries are computed in the aligned coordinates. While this approach is effective for visualization, it has limitations as an inferential primitive. First, the alignment depends on an arbitrary reference configuration, which is not part of the model. Second, the alignment transforms each draw in a draw-specific manner, and the resulting summary is generally not a functional of the posterior distribution alone. Lastly, in multimodal posteriors on the quotient, e.g., when the identifiable structure itself is uncertain, forced alignment can artificially reduce apparent variability or introduce artifacts by collapsing distinct structures into a single coordinate frame.

These observations do not imply that alignment is wrong for plotting. Rather, they imply that alignment should be a visualization step applied after inference is defined intrinsically, the latter of which is not attainable via post hoc alignment. Hence, the discussion above leads to the desirable principle that posterior summaries for LSMs should be defined on a state space that reflects the identifiability structure of the model. Summaries should be functionals of the posterior distribution on this space, and only then may one choose an arbitrary orientation for visualization. In the LSM, the identifiable information is contained in pairwise distances, and centered Gram matrices provide a classical representation

# 3 A Quotient Representation for Latent Space Models

# 3.1 Centered Gram map and invariance

Let X ∈ R n × r and define the centered configuration ˜ X := HX . The associated centered Gram matrix is

$$
B \colon = \Phi ( X ) = \tilde { X } \tilde { X } ^ { \top } = H X X ^ { \top } H .
$$

This representation is classical in distance geometry and multidimensional scaling (Gower and Ross; 1969; Borg and Groenen; 1997). The centered Gram map has three basic properties: for any t ∈ R r and R ∈ O ( r ),

$$
\Phi ( X + 1 _ { n } t ^ { \top } ) = \Phi ( X ) , \quad \Phi ( X R ) = \Phi ( X ) ,
$$

and, for all i, j , Euclidean embedding operator associated with distances, which makes it a natural object for inference when distances are identifiable. Equation (4) is the standard classical MDS identity, whose derivation is given in the Supplementary Material for completeness.

$$

Thus the map Φ removes nuisance symmetries while preserving the likelihood-relevant geometry exactly.

# 3.3 Identifiability and the quotient state space

The centered Gram map Φ( X ) = HXX ⊤ H removes the rigid-motion non-identifiabilities of LSMs while preserving the pairwise geometry that enters the likelihood. Once centered, the Gram matrix identifies the latent configuration up to the orthogonal action that remains unidentifiable under isotropic priors. The next lemma formalizes this point.

Lemma 1 (Gram characterization) . Let X , W ∈ R n × r and write ˜ X = HX , ˜ W = HW . Suppose rank( ˜ X ) = rank( ˜ W ) = r . Then

$$
\tilde { X } \tilde { X } ^ { \top } = \tilde { W } \tilde { W } ^ { \top } \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \tilde { X } = \tilde { W } R \quad \text {for some } R \in O ( r ) .
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\Phi ( X ) = \Phi ( W ) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad X = W R + 1 _ { n } t ^ { \top } \quad f o r \ s o m e \ ( R , t ) \in O ( r ) \times \mathbb { R } ^ { r } .
$$

Lemma 1 shows that Φ( X ) captures exactly the rigid-motion equivalence class of X under the full-rank condition. This motivates viewing posterior inference for LSMs as inference on the image of Φ, rather than on arbitrary coordinate representatives.

We therefore define the inferential state space of identifiable latent structure by

$$
\mathcal { M } _ { n , r } \coloneqq \left \{ B \in \mathbb { R } ^ { n \times n } \ \colon \ B \succeq 0 , \, \text { rank} ( B ) \leq r , \, B 1 _ { n } = 0 \right \} .
$$

The rank constraint rank( B ) ≤ r makes M n,r closed, which is convenient for existence results such as Fr´echet means. The rankr stratum

$$
\mathcal { M } _ { n , r } ^ { \circ } \colon = \{ B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } \colon \, \text {rank} ( B ) = r \}
$$

is a smooth manifold and is the typical support of draws from an r -dimensional latent space model. Every B ∈ M ◦ n,r admits a factorization B = Y Y ⊤ with Y ∈ R n × r , Y ⊤ 1 n = 0 , and rank( Y ) = r , unique up to right multiplication by an orthogonal matrix: Y ∼ Y R for R ∈ O ( r ). This quotient structure is the geometric manifestation of rotational and reflectional non-identifiability.

For an LSM with latent dimension r , every draw automatically satisfies rank(Φ( X ( m ) )) ≤ r . Allowing rank deficiency in (5) therefore avoids boundary issues while remaining faithful to what the model can generate. When needed, one may assume posterior mass concentrates on M ◦ n,r away from the rank-deficient boundary.

# 3.4 Quotient distance and computation

We next equip the space of identifiable latent structures with a distance intrinsic to the quotient representation. Every B ∈ M n,r is centered and positive semidefinite with rank( B ) ≤ r , and hence admits a factorization

$$
B = Y Y ^ { \top } , \quad Y \in \mathbb { R } ^ { n \times r } .
$$

Moreover, the centering constraint B 1 n = 0 allows us to choose a centered factor without loss of generality. Indeed, for any factor Y ,

$$
( H Y ) ( H Y ) ^ { \top } = H Y Y ^ { \top } H = H B H = B \quad \text {and} \quad ( H Y ) ^ { \top } 1 _ { n } = Y ^ { \top } ( H 1 _ { n } ) = 0 .
$$

Thus we represent B throughout using factors satisfying Y ⊤ 1 n = 0 . When rank( B ) < r , some columns of Y may be redundant or zero.

Given B 1 , B 2 ∈ M n,r with B k = Y k Y ⊤ k , one defines the quotient distance

$$
d ( B _ { 1 } , B _ { 2 } ) \colon = \min _ { R \in O ( r ) } \| Y _ { 1 } - Y _ { 2 } R \| _ { F } .
$$

This depends only on B 1 and B 2 where the minimization over O ( r ) removes the remaining gauge freedom in the factorization. Indeed, the minimizer in (6) is obtained by a small orthogonal Procrustes problem (Gower and Dijksterhuis; 2004). Let Y ⊤ 2 Y 1 = U Σ V ⊤ be a singular value decomposition. Then

$$
R ^ { * } = U V ^ { T } , \quad d ( B _ { 1 } , B _ { 2 } ) = \| Y _ { 1 } - Y _ { 2 } R ^ { * } \| _ { F } .
$$

If Y ⊤ 2 Y 1 is rank deficient, R ⋆ need not be unique, but the minimum value is. Equivalently,

$$
d ^ { 2 } ( B _ { 1 } , B _ { 2 } ) = \| Y _ { 1 } \| _ { F } ^ { 2 } + \| Y _ { 2 } \| _ { F } ^ { 2 } - 2 \, t r ( \Sigma ) ,
$$

so the distance depends only on the singular values of Y ⊤ 2 Y 1 . Since r is typically small, e.g., r = 2 or 3 for visualization, computing this distance is inexpensive: forming Y ⊤ 2 Y 1 costs O ( nr 2 ) and the r × r SVD costs O ( r 3 ).

# 3.5 Geometric operations on the rankr stratum

The quotient distance (6) is defined on the closed space M n,r , including points with rank( B ) < r . For the posterior summaries developed later, local linearization is most naturally formulated on the smooth rankr stratum

$$
\mathcal { M } _ { n , r } ^ { \circ } \colon = \{ B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } \colon \, \text {rank} ( B ) = r \} ,
$$

where the quotient structure is smooth and factor representatives have full column rank. On M ◦ n,r , we work with the total space

$$
\bar { y } _ { n , r } \colon = \{ Y \in \mathbb { R } ^ { n \times r } \colon \, Y ^ { \top } 1 _ { n } = 0 , \, r a n k ( Y ) = r \} ,
$$

and identify B = Y Y ⊤ under the equivalence Y ∼ Y R for R ∈ O ( r ). Thus M ◦ n,r is the quotient of ¯ Y n,r under the right action of O ( r ). The full-rank condition ensures that S := Y ⊤ Y is positive definite, which in turn makes the Lyapunov equation used below well posed. A tangent vector at Y is any Z ∈ R n × r satisfying the linearized centering constraint Z ⊤ 1 n = 0 .

The orthogonal gauge invariance induces the vertical space

$$
\mathcal { V } _ { Y } = \{ Y \Omega \colon \Omega ^ { \top } = - \Omega \in \mathbb { R } ^ { r \times r } \} ,
$$

which consists of directions that do not change B = Y Y ⊤ . Under the Frobenius inner product, a standard horizontal space is

$$
\mathcal { H } _ { Y } = \{ Z \colon Z ^ { \top } 1 _ { n } = 0 , \, Y ^ { \top } Z \text { is symmetric} \} ,

whose elements represent identifiable variation in B .

# 4 Posterior Summaries

This section develops posterior summaries for LSMs that are coherent with the quotient representation introduced in Section 3. Let X (1) ,..., X ( M ) denote posterior draws of the latent configuration from Π( ·| A ), and let α (1) ,...,α ( M ) denote the corresponding intercept draws when needed. Each latent draw induces a centered Gram matrix

$$
B ^ { ( m ) } \colon = \Phi ( X ^ { ( m ) } ) = H X ^ { ( m ) } X ^ { ( m ) \top } H , \quad m = 1 , \dots , M ,
$$

which is invariant under rigid motions and preserves all pairwise distances. Defining

$$
Y ^ { ( m ) } \coloneqq H X ^ { ( m ) } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { n \times r } , \quad ( Y ^ { ( m ) } ) ^ { \top } 1 _ { n } = 0 ,
$$

we can equivalently write B ( m ) = Y ( m ) ( Y ( m ) ) ⊤ . Throughout, we treat these draws as given and focus on coherent post-processing of MCMC output. Thus the contribution here is a posterior-analysis layer that can be combined with any MCMC routine for LSMs.

Two complementary modes of inference are useful. The first is intrinsic Monte Carlo inference, which works directly with the empirical distribution of { B ( m ) } M m =1 or its functionals. The second is local linearization on the rankr stratum M ◦ n,r , which provides tractable second-order approximations to posterior variability. Both are defined without using coordinate alignment as an inferential primitive.

A natural posterior point summary should represent the center of the induced posterior on ( M n,r ,d ). Since Euclidean means are characterized as minimizers of sums of squared distances, we define the sample Fr´echet mean of identifiable structure by

$$
\widehat { B } _ { F } \in \arg \min _ { B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } } \ \frac { 1 } { M } \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } d ^ { 2 } ( B , B ^ { ( m ) } ) .
$$

By construction,   B F is invariant under rigid motions and depends only on the empirical posterior distribution of B .

For computation, it is convenient to work with factor representatives. Writing B = Y Y ⊤ with Y ⊤ 1 n = 0 , the Fre´chet objective becomes

$$
F ( Y ) = \frac { 1 } { M } \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } \min _ { R _ { m } \in O ( r ) } \| Y - Y ^ { ( m ) } R _ { m } \| _ { F } ^ { 2 } , \quad \text {subject to } Y ^ { \top } 1 _ { n } = 0 .
$$

Although not jointly convex, F is smooth on the rankr stratum in a quotient sense and can be handled by first-order methods.

Starting from a current iterate Y , we solve for each m ∈ [ M ] the orthogonal Procrustes problem

$$
R _ { m } ^ { * } \in \arg \min _ { R \in O ( r ) } \| Y - Y ^ { ( m ) } R \| _ { F } ,
$$

whose solution is obtained via an SVD of ( Y ( m ) ) ⊤ Y . We then compute

$$
\bar { Y } \coloneqq \frac { 1 } { M } \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } Y ^ { ( m ) } R _ { m } ^ { * } , \quad Z \coloneqq \bar { Y } - Y .
$$

Because the factorization is defined only up to right-orthogonal transformations, Z contains nonidentifiable components. We remove these by horizontal projection, Z hor := Proj hor Y ( Z ), obtained from the Lyapunov equation (8), and update via the retraction (9) Y + = Retr Y ( η Z hor ) , with step size η > 0. After convergence, the posterior mean is reported as   B F = ˆ Y ˆ Y ⊤ . A full algorithmic description is provided in the Supplementary Material.

After defining an intrinsic posterior mean, we quantify uncertainty in two ways: a global dispersion measure and a local second-order approximation around B F .

A global intrinsic dispersion measure is the sample Fre´chet variation,

$$
\widehat { V a r } _ { F } \coloneqq \frac { 1 } { M } \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } d ^ { 2 } ( \widehat { B } _ { F } , B ^ { ( m ) } ) ,
$$

the attained minimum of the Fre´chet objective. This summarizes overall posterior spread around B F without reference to any coordinate system.

A natural global credible region is the intrinsic ball

$$
\mathcal { C } _ { 1 - \alpha } \colon = \{ B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } \colon \, d ( B , \stackrel { . } { B } _ { F } ) \leq r _ { 1 - \alpha } \} ,
$$

where r 1 − α is chosen so that Π B ( C 1 − α | A ) = 1 − α . Using MCMC draws, we estimate it by the empirical quantile

$$
r _ { 1 - \alpha } \colon = Q u a n t i l e _ { 1 - \alpha } \left ( d ( \widehat { B } _ { F } , B ^ { ( 1 ) } ) , \dots , d ( \widehat { B } _ { F } , B ^ { ( M ) } ) \right ) .
$$

For local approximations, we work on the smooth rankr stratum M ◦ n,r . Let ˆ Y be the factor representative so that B F = ˆ Y ˆ Y ⊤ . For each draw Y ( m ) , define the tangent residual

$$
\xi ^ { ( m ) } \colon = L o g _ { \hat { Y } } ( Y ^ { ( m ) } ) \in \mathcal { H } _ { \hat { Y } } .
$$

On M ◦ n,r , the log lift is compatible with the quotient distance: ∥ ξ ( m ) ∥ F = d (   B F , B ( m ) ). Thus the residuals { ξ ( m ) } provide a symmetry-respecting local coordinate system for posterior variation.

We define the empirical tangent covariance operator by

$$
\widehat { \Sigma } \coloneqq \frac { 1 } { M - 1 } \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { M } \xi ^ { ( m ) } \otimes \xi ^ { ( m ) } .
$$

Its effective dimension is that of the rankr stratum, dim( M ◦ n,r ) = nr − r ( r +1) 2 . Diagonalizing   Σ yields dominant directions of local posterior variation, which can be mapped back to the manifold, producing principal geodesic-type visualizations (Fletcher et al.; 2004).

# 4.3 Pairwise distance inference

Pairwise distances are the basic inferential targets in LSMs because they determine edge probabilities in (1). For any B ∈ M n,r , define

$$
D _ { i j } ^ { 2 } ( B ) \coloneqq B _ { i i } + B _ { j j } - 2 B _ { i j } , \quad D _ { i j } ( B ) \coloneqq \sqrt { D _ { i j } ^ { 2 } ( B ) } .
$$

These quantities are intrinsic because they depend only on B .

The most direct approach is Monte Carlo inference. For each pair ( i,j ), compute D ( m ) ij := D ij ( B ( m ) ) and summarize the empirical distribution { D ( m ) ij } M m =1 by posterior means, medians, credible intervals, or tail probabilities.

Distances enter the likelihood through the link-scale term α − ∥ x i − x j ∥ = α − D ij ( B ). Intrinsic posterior inference for edge probabilities therefore propagates the posterior of ( α, B ) through

$$
p _ { i j } ( \alpha , B ) \colon = g ( \alpha - D _ { i j } ( B ) ) \, .
$$

With posterior draws { ( α ( m ) , B ( m ) ) } M m =1 , we compute

$$
p _ { i j } ^ { ( m ) } \colon = g \left ( \alpha ^ { ( m ) } - D _ { i j } ( B ^ { ( m ) } ) \right ) .
$$

The same quantities support posterior predictive checks. Visualization is often central to the interpretation of LSMs, but it should be separated from inference because coordinate orientation is not identifiable. Once intrinsic summaries such as   B F , credible sets, and { U i } have been computed, one may choose any convenient representative embedding for display.

# 4.7 Computational considerations

The computational cost of the proposed summaries is driven mainly by repeated low-rank operations on the factors Y ( m ) . A single evaluation of d ( B 1 , B 2 ) requires forming Y ⊤ 2 Y 1

and computing an SVD of an r × r matrix, with costs O ( nr 2 ) and O ( r 3 ), respectively. Since r is typically small, the dominant cost is O ( nr 2 ).

A full iteration costs O ( Mnr 2 + Mr 3 ), plus the O ( r 3 ) cost of solving the Lyapunov equation (8). Memory usage is dominated by storing { Y ( m ) } rather than { B ( m ) } , requiring O ( Mnr ) rather than O ( Mn 2 ) memory.

In practice, centering should be enforced on factors by applying H to mitigate numerical drift. When Gram matrices are formed explicitly, symmetry can be enforced by replacing B with ( B + B ⊤ ) / 2. If posterior draws approach the rank-deficient boundary, one may regularize computations by truncating very small eigenvalues or restricting local linearization to draws that remain in the rankr stratum.

# 5 Theory

We now establish basic properties of the quotient-based posterior summaries introduced above. Let Π( · | A ) denote the posterior distribution on the latent configuration X , and let Π B ( · | A ) be its pushforward through the centered Gram map Φ, that is, the induced posterior distribution of B = Φ( X ) supported on M n,r . We write ( M n,r ,d ) for the metric space defined by (5) and (6). To distinguish population-level objects from their empirical approximations based on MCMC output, we use B F for a population Fr´echet mean of the induced posterior and reserve B F for the sample Fr´echet mean from Section 4.

# 5.1 Canonicality

We call a posterior summary canonical if it is a measurable functional of Π B ( · | A ). Since the map Φ removes the rigid-motion non-identifiabilities, any measurable functional of the induced posterior on M n,r is automatically invariant to those symmetries.

Theorem 2 (Canonicality) . Any measurable functional of Π B ( · | A ) is invariant under rigid motions of the latent coordinates and hence is canonical. In particular, the population Fr´echet mean

$$
B _ { F } \in \arg \min _ { B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } } \int d ^ { 2 } ( B , B ^ { \prime } ) \, \Pi _ { B } ( d B ^ { \prime } \, | \, A ) ,
$$

the corresponding Fre´chet variation, intrinsic credible balls, distance-based summaries, probability-based summaries, and node-level uncertainty indices are canonical.

The empirical analogues obtained from MCMC output are canonical with respect to the corresponding empirical induced posterior. In many Bayesian LSMs, translation is not a posterior symmetry because the prior anchors the configuration near the origin, whereas orthogonal invariance typically persists; it is this remaining nonidentifiability that the quotient representation resolves.

# 5.2 Existence of the posterior Fre´chet mean

Since M n,r allows rank deficiency, it is closed. This avoids the boundary pathology that would arise if one minimized only over the rank-exact stratum M ◦ n,r . The next result shows that the induced posterior admits a Fre´chet mean under a finite second-moment condition.

Theorem 3 (Existence) . Assume that Π B ( · | A ) has finite second moment with respect to d , that is,

$$
\int d ^ { 2 } ( B , B _ { 0 } ) \, \Pi _ { B } ( d B \, | \, A ) < \infty \quad \text {for some } B _ { 0 } \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } .
$$

Then there exists at least one Fre´chet mean

$$
B _ { F } \in \arg \min _ { B \in \mathcal { M } _ { n , r } } \int d ^ { 2 } ( B , B ^ { \prime } ) \, \Pi _ { B } ( d B ^ { \prime } \, | \, A ) .
$$

Theorem 3 guarantees existence on the closed space M n,r , but not necessarily rank exactly r . If posterior mass accumulates near the rank-deficient boundary, a Fr´ echet mean may also lie there. A simple sufficient condition for B F ∈ M ◦ n,r is that the posterior be supported away from the boundary, e.g., if λ r ( B ) ≥ ϵ > 0 almost surely for some ϵ > 0.

# 5.3 Stability under perturbations of the posterior

A coherent posterior summary should vary continuously under small perturbations of the posterior law. This matters conceptually, and also practically because exact posteriors are typically approximated by finite MCMC output.

Let P 2 ( M n,r ) denote the set of probability measures on M n,r with finite second moment under d . For P ∈ P 2 ( M n,r ), define

$$

$$

# 7 Conclusion

This paper argues that posterior analysis for LSMs should be carried out on identifiable latent structure rather than on arbitrary coordinate representatives. The centered Gram map provides a natural quotient representation for this purpose: it removes rigid-motion nonidentifiability while preserving exactly the pairwise geometry that drives the likelihood. This viewpoint leads to canonical posterior summaries that depend only on the induced posterior distribution on the quotient space, not on an external alignment convention.

Within this framework, we introduced an intrinsic posterior mean through a Fre´chet criterion, together with global dispersion, intrinsic credible regions, dyad-level summaries for distances and edge probabilities, and node-level uncertainty indices. These quantities are computable directly from MCMC output using only small orthogonal Procrustes problems and low-rank matrix operations. We also established basic theoretical guarantees, including canonicality, existence of Fr´echet means on a closed state space, stability un-

The empirical results reinforce the main methodological message. In the simulation study, alignment-based summaries were broadly similar to quotient-based summaries when the posterior was well concentrated, but became increasingly reference-dependent as identification weakened. The Florentine marriage network showed that quotient-based uncertainty can reveal ambiguous family positions and latent affinities not evident from a single embedding alone. The coauthorship example demonstrated that the same framework remains informative in a larger contemporary network, where node-level and dyad-level uncertainty helped clarify porous community boundaries and boundary-spanning collaboration patterns.

Several extensions merit further study. One direction is to adapt the framework to directed, weighted, bipartite, or dynamic LSMs. Another is to develop analogous quotientbased posterior summaries for non-Euclidean latent spaces such as hyperbolic or spherical models. On the computational side, large-scale approximations and streaming implementations would be valuable. Because the present framework operates as a post-processing layer on posterior draws, improving its scalability would broaden the practical reach of coherent uncertainty quantification in latent network models.

The main practical conclusion is simple: alignment remains useful for visualization, but it should follow inference rather than define it. By separating inferential content from arbitrary orientation, the quotient perspective yields posterior summaries that are both interpretable and faithful to the symmetry structure of the model.

# Coauthorship Preprocessing Details

The coauthorship analysis begins from the MATLAB file CoauAdjFinal.mat , which stores an adjacency matrix A together with the corresponding author names. This file is produced from the author–paper incidence data in the MADStat repository available at https: //github.com/ZhengTracyKe/MADStat by first forming the weighted coauthorship matrix A wt = XX ⊤ , where X is the author–paper incidence matrix, then thresholding the weights so that

A ij = 1 ⇐⇒ authors i and j coauthored at least three papers ,

and setting A ii = 0. The resulting thresholded graph is then restricted to its largest connected component before being saved in CoauAdjFinal.mat . In our case, this network contains 4,383 authors.

Community labels are taken from the companion file CommunityResults firstlayer.mat , which stores the first-layer partition used in Ji et al. (2022). These labels are obtained by applying SCORE to the adjacency matrix with diagonal regularization and then relabeling the six detected communities to match the interpretation in that paper. In the R implementation, the author ordering in CoauAdjFinal.mat and CommunityResults firstlayer.mat was checked using the stored authorNames fields, and all subsequent preprocessing was carried out only after verifying that the node orderings agreed.

We then restricted attention to the three first-layer biostatistics communities with labels 2, 4, and 6, corresponding to Biostatistics (Europe), Biostatistics (UNC), and Biostatistics (Michigan), respectively. Their sizes are 202, 673, and 264, yielding an induced subgraph on 1,139 authors. This induced subgraph is disconnected: it contains 64 connected components, of which the largest has 980 nodes. All subsequent model fitting and posterior analysis are based on this 980-node component.

For computational feasibility, the final fitted network was obtained by trimming the 980node component to a connected 400-node subgraph. In the analysis code, nodes were ranked by degree within the 980-node component, the highest-degree 400 nodes were retained, and the largest connected component of this retained subgraph was used for model fitting. The latent space model and all quotient-based summaries reported in the main text are therefore based on this final connected 400-node network.
