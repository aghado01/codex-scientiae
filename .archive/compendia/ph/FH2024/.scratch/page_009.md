(ii) The colimit of \( M \) is (isomorphic to) the pair \( ( C, ( i_p )_{p \in P} ) \) described as follows: for \( p \in P \) let the map \( j_p : M_p \to \bigoplus_{p \in P} M_p \) be the canonical injection. \( C \) is the quotient vector space \( ( \bigoplus_{p \in P} M_p ) /T \), where \( T \) is the subspace of \( \bigoplus_{p \in P} M_p \) which is generated by vectors of the form \( j_p ( v_p ) - j_q ( v_q ) \) for \( v_p \sim v_q \) and the maps \( i_p : M_p \to C \) are the compositions \( \rho \circ j_p \), where \( \rho \) is the quotient map \( \rho : \bigoplus_{p \in P} M_p \to C \).

Definition 2.17 A path in \( P \) between \( p \in P \) and \( q \in P \) is a sequence of elements \( p = p_1, \dots, p_l = q \) in \( P \) such that for all \( 1 \leq i \leq l - 1 \) the elements \( p_i \) and \( p_{i+1} \) are comparable. A section along the path \( \Gamma \) is an \( l \)-tuple \( v \in \bigoplus_{i=1}^l M_{p_i} \) with the property that \( v_{p_i} \sim v_{p_{i+1}} \) for all \( i = 1, \dots, l - 1 \).

The following proposition will be utilized in the proof of Theorem 2.23. We use the same notation as in Proposition 2.16.

Proposition 2.18 [9] For \( p, q \in P \) and vectors \( v_p \in M_p \) and \( v_q \in M_q \), it holds that \( [ j_p ( v_p )] = [ j_q ( v_q )] \in \lim_{\longrightarrow} M \) if there exists a path \( \Gamma \) between \( p \) and \( q \) and a section \( ( w_p )_{p \in \Gamma} \) of \( M \) along \( \Gamma \) such that \( w_p = v_p \) and \( w_q = v_q \).

Proof: It follows directly from the explicit formula for the colimit in Proposition 2.16. □

To keep notations simple, we write \( [ v_p ] \) instead of \( [ j_p ( v_p )] \) for elements in the colimit.

Remark 2.19 Note that the converse of Proposition 2.18 does not hold in general. Consider for example the module

$$
\[
\begin{CD}
\mathbb{F}_2 @>{\binom{1}{0}}>> \mathbb{F}_2^2 \\
@V{id}VV @VVV \\
\mathbb{F}_2 @>{\binom{1}{1}}>> \mathbb{F}_2
\end{CD}
\]
$$

Passing to the colimit, in the upper right vector space \( \binom{1}{0} = \binom{0}{1} \) holds, because there exists a section along the closed path that identifies the two vectors, and hence \( \binom{1}{1} = \binom{0}{0} \). On the other hand, the vector \( \binom{1}{1} \in \mathbb{F}_2^2 \) in the upper right vector space does not lie in the image of any of the arrows and thus, there exists no section along any path such that the above holds.

The following definition of lower and upper fences will be useful in several places in this article. In particular, these are subsets that carry the important property that they already completely determine the limits and colimits of a diagram.

Definition 2.20 A subposet \( L \subset P \) is called a lower fence of \( P \) if \( L \) is connected and for any \( q \in P \) the intersection \( L \cap \{ r \in P : r \leq q \} \) is nonempty and connected.

Analogously, a subposet \( U \subset P \) is called an upper fence of \( P \) if \( U \) is connected and for any \( q \in P \) the intersection \( U \cap \{ r \in P : q \leq r \} \) is nonempty and connected.

The following Proposition already appears among others in [9] and will be used in the proof of the main theorem of this Section.

Proposition 2.21 Let \( L \) and \( U \) be lower and upper fences, respectively. Given any \( P \) indexed persistence module \( M \), we have that \( \lim_{\longleftarrow} M \cong \lim_{\longleftarrow} M|_L \) and \( \lim_{\longrightarrow} M \cong \lim_{\longrightarrow} M|_U \).
