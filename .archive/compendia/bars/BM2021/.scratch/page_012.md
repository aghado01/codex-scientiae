As mentioned above, the value $c_0 = 1.54764$ ensures Fisher-consistency of the scale estimator when the errors have a normal distribution. Besides, $c_1 = 4.685$ corresponds to the tuning constant that guarantees, for normal errors, a 95% efficiency for the robust estimators of $\beta$ (see Theorem 4.1 and Maronna et al., 2019). We have also evaluated the quantile estimator defined in Guo et al. (2013) taking a 0.5 quantile, that is, we chose $\tau = 1/2$ in the loss function $\rho_\tau(u) = u(\tau - I(u < 0))$ defining the estimators. This choice ensures that, for clean samples, the three procedures estimate the same quantities; otherwise the estimator of $\mu$ will be biased. Note that, when $\tau = 0.5$, the quantile estimators minimize the absolute value of the residuals, corresponding to the so-called $L_1$ or least absolute deviation estimators. From now on, we will denote them as $\ell_1$ in all tables and figures. Besides, the classical least squares estimator will be labeled as $\ell s$ in all tables and figures and the robust MM-procedure proposed in this paper as $mm$.

In all scenarios, we performed $N = 500$ replications, the sample size was $n = 100$ and we used cubic splines with equally spaced knots for all nonparametric components. For numerical simplicity, we also chose the same number of terms in the spline approximation for each additive component, that is, $k_j = k$ for all $j$. As in He et al. (2002), the common number of elements in the basis $k$ varies between $\max\{n^{1/5}/2; 4\}$ and $8 + 2n^{1/5}$, which for the sample size considered leads to $4 \leq k \leq 13$. To select the basis dimension, we minimized the RBIC criterion defined in (8) over the set $\{4, \ldots, 13\}$ with the same $\rho$-function considered in the estimation step. Hence, for the robust estimator, the $\rho$-function in (8) is the Tukey's bisquare loss function with tuning constant $c_1 = 4.685$, while for the classical and $L_1$ estimators, $\rho(t) = t^2$ and $\rho(t) = |t|$, respectively. Note that for the classical and $L_1$ procedures no scale estimator is needed.

The samples $(Y_i, Z_i^t, X_i^t)^t_{i=1}^n$ are generated with the same distribution as $(Y, Z^t, X^t)^t$, with $X = (X_1, X_2)^t \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and $Z = (Z_1, Z_2)^t \in \mathbb{R}^2$. In all cases, the response and the covariates are related through the partially linear additive model $Y = \mu + \beta^t Z + \eta_1(X_1) + \eta_2(X_2) + U$, where $U = \sigma\varepsilon$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $\beta = (\beta_1, \beta_2)^t = (3, 3)^t$, $\mu = 0$, $\eta_1(x_1) = 2\sin(\pi x_1) - 4/\pi$ and $\eta_2(x_2) = e^{x_2} - (e - 1)$, ensuring that $\int_0^1 \eta_1(x)\,dx = \int_0^1 \eta_2(x)\,dx = 0$. Six possible models combining different choices for covariates distributions were studied. For all models $X_1$ and $X_2$ have marginal uniform distribution but different correlation between the covariates is allowed. In Models 4 to 6, $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ have a discrete distribution while in Models 1 to 3, they correspond to continuous random variables. For clean samples, denoted from now on as $C_0$, the errors distribution is $\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1)$ and the considered models for the covariates distribution are

- **Model 1:** $Z_1, Z_2, X_1$ and $X_2$ are i.i.d. $U(0, 1)$.
- **Model 2:** $Z_1, Z_2, X_1$ and $X_2$ have marginal $U(0, 1)$ distribution, $X_1$ and $Z_1$ have correlation 0.7, while $Z_2$ and $X_2$ are independent and independent of $Z_1$ and $X_1$.
- **Model 3:** $X_1$ and $X_2$ are i.i.d. $U(0, 1)$ and we define $Z_1 = X_1 + X_2/2 + v_1$, $Z_2 = \{\exp(X_1) - 1\}/2 + v_2$, where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are i.i.d. $v_j \sim N(0, (0.1)^2)$.
- **Model 4:** $X_1$ and $X_2$ are i.i.d. with distribution $U(0, 1)$, $Z_1 = W_1/3$ and $Z_2 = W_2/5$ where $W_1 \sim \mathrm{Bi}(3, 1/2)$ and $W_2 \sim \mathrm{Bi}(5, 1/5)$ are independent of each other.
- **Model 5:** $X_1$ and $X_2$ are i.i.d. $X_j \sim U(0, 1)$, $Z_1 = W_1/10$ and $Z_2 = W_2/10$ with $(W_1, W_2, W_3) \sim \mathrm{M}(10, 1/4, 1/2, 1/4)$, that is, $(W_1, W_2, W_3)$ have a joint multinomial distribution of size 10 and success probabilities $1/4$, $1/2$ and $1/4$. Besides, $X_1$, $X_2$ and $(W_1, W_2, W_3)$ are independent.
- **Model 6:** $X_1$ and $X_2$ are i.i.d. $X_j \sim U(0, 1)$, $Z_1 = W_1/5$ with $W_1 \sim \mathrm{Bi}(5, 1/4)$, $X_1$, $X_2$ and $W_1$ are independent. Moreover, $Z_2 = (1/2)\{I_{(0;\,2/3)}(X_1) + W\}$ with $W \sim \mathrm{Bi}(1, 1/2)$ independent of $X_1$.

Note that all considered models satisfy the additivity required in assumption N3. However, under Model 6, $h_2^*(x) = (1/2)I_{(0;\,2/3)}(x_1) + (1/4)$ is not a smooth function.

To study the effect of atypical data on the estimators, we considered three contamination schemes which are described as

- $C_1$: $U_i \sim 0.9\,N(0, \sigma^2) + 0.1\,N(0, 100\sigma^2)$.
- $C_2$: $U_i \sim 0.85\,N(0, \sigma^2) + 0.15\,N(15, 0.1^2)$.
- $C_3$: The square $[0,1]^2$ is divided into 9 equally-sized quadrants as shown in Fig. 1. In this scenario the errors are not contaminated, that is, $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1)$, but artificially 9 observations were modified in such a way that $(Z_{1i}, Z_{2i})^t = (20, 20)^t$ and its related pair $(X_{1i}, X_{2i})^t$ of covariates belonged to a different quadrant in the square $[0,1] \times [0,1]$.

Contaminations $C_1$ and $C_2$ correspond to vertical outliers and it is expected that they will affect mainly the estimation of $\mu$ and eventually that of the additive components. In particular, $C_1$ corresponds to a 10% of the errors with a larger variance, producing an increase in the mean square error but not in the bias of the estimates. In contrast, scenario $C_2$ corresponds to an asymmetric gross error model in which a 15% of the errors are shifted in order to produce "vertical outliers" and will have more effect on the bias. Finally, contamination $C_3$ aims to affect the regression parameter also through the high-leverage points introduced. Fig. 1 shows the 100 pairs of $X_i$ for one sample with $X_1$ and $X_2$ following a $U(0,1)$ distribution. The solid blue circles correspond to the covariates $X_i$ where $Z_i$ has been contaminated. When no generated data points $X_i$ were found in a quadrant, the observation $Z_i$ was not contaminated, so the total amount of contaminated observations may be smaller than 9.
