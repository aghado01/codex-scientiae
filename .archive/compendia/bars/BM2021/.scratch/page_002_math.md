The   particular   situation   where   X ∈ R is   considered   in   Härdle   et   al.   ( 2000 ) and   Härdle   et   al.   ( 2004 ) who   describe   different   procedures   based   on   kernels   or   splines   to   estimate   the   unknown   quantities.   As   in   linear   regression   models,   these   estimators   are   very   sensitive   to   atypical   observations   since   they   are   based   on   the   least   squares   principle.   When   p   = 1 and   to   deal   with   more   reliable   procedures   when   atypical   data   arise,   Bianco   and   Boente   ( 2004 ) considered   robust   estimators   based   on   local   M -estimators,   while   He   and   Shi   ( 1996 ) and   He   et   al.   ( 2002 ) studied   M -estimators   based   on   splines.

It   is   worth   mentioning   that,   when   p   > 1,   model   ( 1 ) requires   multivariate   smoothing,   so   that   the   “curse   of   dimensionality” is   not   overcome   specially   when   p   ≥ 4.   Partially   linear   additive   regression   models   ( plam )   provide   an   attempt   to   solve   this   problem,   since   the   covariates   related   to   the   nonparametric   component   enter   the   model   through   an   additive   structure,   leading   to   the   relationship

Usually,   the   functions   η j are   assumed   to   be   continuous   with   support   on   a   compact   interval   I j which   is   also   the   support   of   the   distribution   of   X j. As   mentioned   in   Ma   ( 2012 ),   model   ( 2 ) is   more   parsimonious   and   easier   to   interpret   than   purely   additive   models   and   more   ﬂexible   than   linear   models.   In   this   sense,   the   advantage   over   fully   nonparametric   models,   where   the   covariates   are   usually   assumed   to   have   a   density   with   compact   support,   is   that   through   a   partially   linear   additive   regression   model   the   practitioner   may   include   in   the   linear   regression   component   covariates   which   are   discrete,   dummy   or   unbounded.   Besides   the   situation   where   a   subset   or   all   the   variables   in   Z are   discrete,   partially   linear   additive   regression   models   are   suitable   when   the   practitioner   is   fairly   certain   of   the   linear   relation   existing   between   the   response   and   some   covariates,   but   not   about   the   shape   of   the   relationship   with   the   other   covariates.   We   refer   to   Opsomer   and   Ruppert   ( 1999 ) for   a   discussion   on   this   topic.

Stone   ( 1985 ) discussed   different   issues,   such   as   model   dimensionality   and   ﬂexibility,   for   different   nonparametric   and   semiparametric   models   including   the   partially   linear   additive   regression   model   ( 2 ).   To   estimate   the   components   of   the   model,   Fan   et   al.   ( 1998 ) introduced   two   least   squares   kernel   approaches,   which   have   a   high   computational   cost,   since   they   perform   sequential   estimations   in   the   direction   of   interest,   see   also   Härdle   et   al.   ( 2004 ).   A   related   kernel   based   method   that,   at   the   initial   stage   when   estimating   β,   makes   use   both   of   the   additive   structure   of   the   nonparametric   component   and   of   the   fact   that   Z enters   in   the   regression   function   linearly,   was   considered   in   Fan   and   Li   ( 2003 ).   A   different   family   of   kernel   based   estimators,   that   reduces   the   computational   requirement,   was   studied   in   Manzan   and   Zerom   ( 2005 ),   while   Liang   et   al.   ( 2008 ) considered   the   situation   where   the   linear   covariate   is   measured   with   error.   Following   a   different   approach,   Opsomer   and   Ruppert   ( 1999 ) combined   local   linear   smoothers   and   backﬁtting   to   provide   a   root − n consistent   estimator   of   β.  In   this   direction,   a   robust   algorithm   based   on   backﬁtting   and   the   generalized   Speckman   estimator   was   considered   in   Pan   ( 2013 ) for   generalized   partial   linear   additive   models.   However,   no   asymptotic   properties   were   derived   for   this   procedure.

The   described   methods   do   not   estimate   the   parameters   and   nonparametric   components   simultaneously.   For   that   reason   and   following   the   spline   approach   given   in   Stone   ( 1985 ) for   additive   models,   Li   ( 2000 ) studied   estimators   based   on   general   series   methods,   with   power   series   and   splines   as   a   particular   case.   To   obtain   estimators   of   the   additive   components   whose   asymptotic   distribution   can   be   derived,   Ma   and   Yang   ( 2011 ) introduced   an   approach   that   combines   spline   estimation   and   kernel   methods.   In   the   context   of   high-dimensional   sparse   models,   Liu   et   al.   ( 2011 ) and   Lian   ( 2012 ) developed   a   variable   selection   procedure   based   on   least   squares   regression   and   spline   approximations,   using   SCAD   and   adaptive   LASSO   penalizations   on   the   regression   parameter   β,   respectively.

All   these   estimators   are   based   on   a   least   squares   approach   and   assume   that   the   error   has   ﬁnite   variance,   so,   as   in   partial   linear   models,   a   small   proportion   of   atypical   data   may   seriously   affect   the   estimations.   A   more   resistant   approach   based   on   quantile   regression   and   spline   approximation   was   suggested   in   Guo   et   al.   ( 2013 ) and   Sherwood   and   Wang   ( 2016 ) who   also   considered   variable   selection   in   sparse   models.   An   extension   to   censored   partially   linear   additive   models   was   studied   in   Liu   et   al.   ( 2017 ),   while   an   approach   based   on   penalized   splines   was   discussed   in   Koenker   ( 2011 ).   Note   that   quantile   estimators   are   related   to   an   unbounded   loss   function   and   for   that   reason,   as   in   linear   regression   models,   they   may   be   affected   by   high–leverage   outliers.   In   particular,   when   considering   a   0.5   quantile,   quantile   regression   estimators   are   related   to   the   well   known   least   absolute   deviations   estimators.

To overcome the sensitivity of quantile estimators to high-leverage outliers, we adapt best practices for robust multiple linear regression estimators to partial linear additive models. More precisely, we propose estimators that combine B -splines to approximate the additive components with MM -regression estimators (Yohai, 1987). As it is well known, MM -regression estimators are a family of scale equivariant estimators, robust against high-leverage outliers since they are obtained using a bounded loss function in the minimization problem and a preliminary robust high breakdown point scale estimator. This will allow that, in our approach, the error's distribution may have heavy tails since, instead of requiring E (ε) = 0 and Var (ε) = 1 as in the classical setting, we only require that the error ε has a symmetric distribution F ( · ).The scale parameter of F ( · ) is assumed to be 1 to identify σ.In this way, the resulting estimators are strongly consistent under standard regularity conditions and can be calibrated so that the regression estimators achieve high efficiency for normal errors. Moreover, as the numerical results reported in Section 5 show, our proposal leads to estimators resistant to vertical outliers and to atypical data with high-leverage in the covariates corresponding to the linear component.
