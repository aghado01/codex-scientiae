In this context, it should be mentioned that in many applications categorical variables may arise. The problem on how to deal with discrete variables in fully nonparametric regression problems has been considered in the statistical literature, by several authors. Among others, we can mention the proposals based on splines for the continuous covariates and on discrete-support kernel functions for the categorical ones, considered in Racine and Li (2004) and Ma et al. (2015) for nonparametric regression models and by Ma and Racine (2013) for additive models. As mentioned in Camlong-Viot et al. (2006), who considered a kernel approach combined with marginal integration, in the econometric literature categorical covariates are usually included in the form of linear parametric functions, see also Ma and Yang (2011). Throughout this paper, as in Ma and Yang (2011) who considered a least squares approach or Guo et al. (2013) and Sherwood and Wang (2016) whose proposal is based on quantile regression, we will assume that discrete variables if present are included in the model as some of the components of the vector $Z$ whose relation with the responses is modeled parametrically.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe our robust proposal, while theoretical results regarding consistency and rates of convergence are presented in Section 3. Furthermore, asymptotic normality is established for the estimators of $\beta$ in Section 4. The results of a numerical study conducted to compare the finite-sample properties of the classical least squares and the robust MM-estimator are summarized in Section 5, while Section 6 contains the analysis of the Boston housing data set. Some final comments are presented in Section 7. All proofs are relegated to the Appendix or to the supplementary file available on-line, where some additional numerical results are also included.

## 2. The Robust Estimators

As mentioned in the introduction, we will consider i.i.d. observations $(Y_i, Z_i^t, X_i^t)^t$ satisfying the partially linear additive model (2) where $X_i$ has support on $\prod_{j=1}^{p} I_j$. From now on, given two random vectors $W \in \mathbb{R}^s$ and $V \in \mathbb{R}^s$, we denote $W \sim V$ whenever $W$ and $V$ have the same distribution. In the sequel, $(Y, Z^t, X^t)^t$ stands for a random vector with the same distribution as $(Y_1, Z_1^t, X_1^t)^t$, that is, $(Y, Z^t, X^t)^t \sim (Y_1, Z_1^t, X_1^t)^t$. To ensure identifiability of the additive components $\eta_j : I_j \to \mathbb{R}$, we will impose the constraint $\int_{I_j} \eta_j(x)\,dx = 0$, for $j = 1, \ldots, p$. The errors $\varepsilon$ are assumed to be independent of the explanatory variables $(Z^t, X^t)^t$. Taking into account that we intend to define robust estimators, we avoid moment conditions for the error's distribution by only assuming that their distribution $F(\cdot)$ is symmetric with scale parameter 1. Our robust proposal combines B-splines with regression MM-estimators, that is, the estimators to be defined in Section 2.2 are based on MM-regression estimators after approximating each component $\eta_j$ by a spline as described in Section 2.1. Under a linear regression model, MM-estimators were introduced in Yohai (1987). A detailed description, as well as an algorithm allowing to compute them, can be seen in Maronna et al. (2019). As it is well known, to attain robustness and efficiency, MM-estimators are defined through a two–step procedure. In the first step, a preliminary robust regression estimator is used to estimate the scale parameter. This initial estimator may be inefficient but should have a high breakdown point. In the second step, an M-estimator is computed using a bounded loss function applied to the standardized residuals. In this way, the final estimator will retain the high breakdown point of the initial one but its efficiency will be improved by the use of an appropriate loss function in the second step. The next two sections contain the details of our proposal.

### 2.1. B-spline Approximation of the Additive Components

To approximate each additive function $\eta_j$ in (2) by a spline, fix the desired spline order $\ell_j$, the number of interior knots $N_{n,j}$ and their location $T_j$. As it is well known, the class of splines of order $\ell_j > 1$ with knots $T_j$ is spanned by a B-spline basis functions with dimension $k_j = k_{n,j} = N_{n,j} + \ell_j$, see Corollary 4.10 of Schumaker (1981). We will denote this basis $\{B_s^{(j)} : 1 \leq s \leq k_{n,j}\}$. It is worth mentioning that a spline of order $\ell$ is a polynomial of degree $\ell - 1$ within each subinterval. It should be noted that when using cubic splines, the consistency results derived in Section 3 hold when the additive components are twice continuously differentiable.

To define the estimators, for any vector $\lambda = (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_{k_j})^t \in \mathbb{R}^{k_j}$, we consider a spline $g_j(x) = \sum_{s=1}^{k_j} \lambda_s B_s^{(j)}(x)$ to approximate $\eta_j$, for $1 \leq j \leq p$. It is worth noticing that even when considering the classical least squares estimators, the minimization should be carried out taking into account the constraints $\int_{I_j} g_j(x)\,dx = \sum_{s=1}^{k_j} \lambda_s^{(j)} \int_{I_j} B_s^{(j)}(x)\,dx = 0$, for $j = 1, \ldots, p$. As in Guo et al. (2013), to deal with an unconstrained optimization problem, we center the basis functions, that is, we define $\tilde{B}_s^{(j)}(x) = B_s^{(j)}(x) - \int_{I_j} B_s^{(j)}(x)\,dx$ and the centered approximation candidates for $\eta_j$ as $\sum_{s=1}^{k_j} \lambda_s^{(j)} \tilde{B}_s^{(j)}(x)$.
