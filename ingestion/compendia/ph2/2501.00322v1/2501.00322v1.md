# BIPATH PERSISTENCE AS ZIGZAG PERSISTENCE

ÁNGEL JAVIER ALONSO AND ENHAO LIU

Abstract. Persistence modules that decompose into interval modules are important in topological data analysis because we can interpret such intervals as the lifetime of topological features in the data. We can classify the settings in which persistence modules always decompose into intervals, by a recent result of Aoki, Escolar and Tada: these are standard single-parameter persistence, zigzag persistence, and bipath persistence. No other setting offers such guarantees.

We show that a bipath persistence module can be decomposed via a closely related infinite zigzag persistence module, understood as a covering. This allows us to translate techniques of zigzag persistence, like recent advancements in its efficient computation by Dey and Hou, to bipath persistence. In addition, and again by the relation with the infinite zigzag, we can define an interleaving and bottleneck distance on bipath persistence. In turn, the algebraic stability of zigzag persistence implies the algebraic stability of bipath persistence.

# 1. Introduction

Intervals have an outsized role in persistent homology: we interpret them as the lifetime of topological features in the data. In standard single-parameter persistence, where we consider persistence modules over a totally ordered set, the modules always decompose into interval modules. The intervals in the decomposition are the barcode of the module. The same happens in zigzag persistence, where we consider persistence modules over a zigzag partially ordered set (poset); all modules decompose into interval modules [12]. Even in settings where modules do not always decompose into interval modules, like multiparameter persistence—where the underlying poset is a grid—, intervals are useful in applications. For example, consider the fibered barcode [22], which consists of single-parameter slices of a multiparameter module, and whose intervals, apart from being useful on their own, also give the multiparameter landscape [25]. In this regard, it is unsurprising that much work on multiparameter persistence aims at understanding when multiparameter modules are interval-decomposable [16, 18, 1, 2, 8, 9, 5], or at approximations by using the intervals [21, 22, 6, 7, 13, 14].

We can classify the settings where we always have interval decomposability by a recent result of Aoki, Escolar and Tada [3]: persistence modules over a finite poset always decompose into interval modules if and only if the poset is a totally ordered set (single-parameter persistence), a zigzag poset or a bipath poset. A bipath poset is the disjoint union of two finite totally ordered sets, with a common bottom and top element added; see Figure 1.

One can obtain a bipath module—a persistence module over a bipath posetby an order-preserving map between a bipath poset and another poset, like R 2 ; much like one embeds a totally ordered set to obtain a fiber of the fibered barcode. Given the role they play in understanding persistence modules that always decompose into intervals, we aim to further the theoretical and computational understanding of bipath modules. Such a study has been started by Aoki, Escolar and Tada [3, 4]. They define persistence diagrams in this context, and provide a matrixbased algorithm to decompose bipath persistence modules [4]. Here, among other consequences detailed below, we show that a bipath module can be decomposed by directly decomposing a related (finite) zigzag module. This allows us to use the zigzag decomposition algorithm, in the case of the modules being the homology of a simplex-wise filtration, of Dey and Hou [17], which works by decomposing a related standard single-parameter persistence module. Thus, any improvement to the decomposition of standard persistence transfers to zigzag persistence and then to bipath persistence.

1.1. Contributions. We define a poset map from an infinite zigzag to the bipath, which we call the covering map and is inspired by the universal covering of the circle. See Figure 2 for an illustration.

In Section 3, we establish algebraic stability for bipath persistence. This is a consequence of the algebraic stability of zigzag modules, which is, in turn, a consequence of the algebraic stability of block-decomposable modules [11, 10]. Specifically, we define the interleaving and bottleneck distance between bipath modules M and N to be the interleaving and bottleneck distance between R ( M ) and R ( N ) , as zigzag modules—much like one does for zigzag modules and block-decomposable modules [11, 10].

In Section 4, we observe that to decompose the bipath module one does not need to consider the infinite zigzag module in its entirety, but just a finite slice of it, of size O ( K ) , where K is the maximum length of a chain in the bipath poset.

Finally, in Section 5 we define the fibered arc code for persistence module over R 2 , and compare it with other invariants through examples.

1.2. Related work. Aoki, Emerson and Tada initiated the study of bipath persistence and provided an algorithm to decompose bipath modules and a way to visualize such a decomposition [4]. Their algorithm is based on column and row operations on matrices. In a recent seminar, Tada [24] has mentioned ongoing work on a stability theory for functions from a certain continuous version of bipath posets to topological spaces. A priori, this is unrelated to the algebraic stability theorem we state here, which is a direct consequence of the covering map of Section 2 that relates bipath to zigzag persistence.

Also recently, Asashiba and Liu [8] decompose a bipath module by decomposing two related zigzag modules, inspired by the notion of essential cover that they introduce. In our approach, we use a single zigzag, which, apart from simplifying the computation, allows us to translate the algebraic stability of zigzag modules to this setting, as mentioned.

In the context of circle-valued maps and level persistence, Burghelea and Dey [15] have studied (pointwise finite-dimensional) persistence modules over finite subsets of the circle S 1 equipped with a specific partial order. Such persistence modules decompose as a direct sum of interval modules and Jordan cells. Since their methods can be used to decompose zigzag modules, and as a consequence of our work, their methods could also be applied to decompose bipath modules.

Sala and Schiffmann [23] have studied a similar finite persistence module over S 1 with the cyclic order. Hanson and Rock [20] study continuous pointwise finitedimensional persistence modules over S 1 equipped with certain general partial orders (which include the cyclic order and the bipath order) and provide a decomposition theorem into intervals and Jordan cells. Recently, Gao and Zhao [19] prove an isometry theorem for nilpotent persistence modules over S 1 with the cyclic order.

We consider actions on multisets in the following sense: a group G has an action α on the multiset A if G has an action α : G × S → S on S such that µ ( α ( g,a )) = µ ( a ) . 2.1. Intervals in a bipath. Let us start by describing how bipath modules (that is, persistence modules over the bipath poset) decompose into interval modules, following Aoki, Emerson and Tada [4]. $$

# 3. Algebraic stability

As we have seen in Theorem 2.3, the covering map ζ : ZZ → B allows us to translate the arc code of the bipath module to the barcode of the infinite zigzag module, and vice versa. Furthermore, and as we do shortly, the covering map can be used to study the algebraic stability of bipath modules, via the algebraic stability of zigzag modules, which is well-established following Botnan and Lesnick [11] and Bjerkevik [10]. These stability results depend on the concept of block-decomposable modules, which we review first after recalling the definitions of interleavings and bottleneck distance.

3.1. Interleavings and matchings. We review interleavings over R op × R and the bottleneck distance. Recall that for an ϵ ∈ [0 , ∞ ] , we define the shift functor ( · )( ϵ ): Vec R op × R → Vec R op × R by sending M to the persistence module given by M ( ϵ ) ( x,y ) : = M ( x − ϵ,y + ϵ ) and M ( ϵ ) ( x,y ) → ( w,z ) : = M ( x − ϵ,y + ϵ ) → ( w − ϵ,z + ϵ ) . On morphisms f : M → N , we set f ( ϵ ) ( x,y ) : = f ( x − ϵ,y + ϵ ) . For every module M , we define a morphism ϕ M ϵ : M → M ( ϵ ) by the internal maps M ( x,y ) → ( x − ϵ,y + ϵ ) . We say that two persistence modules M,N ∈ Vec R op × R are ϵ -interleaved if there exist morphisms f : M → N ( ϵ ) and g : N → M ( ϵ ) such that g ( ϵ ) ◦ f = ϕ M 2 ϵ and f ( ϵ ) ◦ g = ϕ N 2 ϵ .

As in [10], we describe matchings in terms of interleavings between interval modules. Let A and B be two multisets of intervals of R op × R . An ϵ -matching between A and B is given by a bijection σ : A ′ → B ′ for some subsets A ′ ⊂ A and B ′ ⊂ B such that

- • every interval I in A \ A ′ and B \ B ′ satisfies that k I is ϵ -interleaved with the zero module, and
- • for every I ∈ A ′ , the modules k I and k σ ( I ) are ϵ -interleaved.


Then, the interleaving distance d I ( M,N ) between two persistence modules M,N : R op × R → Vec and the bottleneck distance d B ( C,D ) between two multisets of intervals C and D are given by

d I ( M,N ) : = inf { ϵ | M and N are ϵ -interleaved } , d B ( C,D ) : = inf { ϵ | there is an ϵ -matching between C and D } .

In the sequel, for two persistence modules M and N , d B ( M,N ) denotes the bottleneck distance between the barcodes B ( M ) and B ( N ) . Namely, d B ( M,N ) : = d B ( B ( M ) , B ( N )) .

- • for every I ∈ C , the modules E ◦ R ( k I ) and E ◦ R ( k σ ( I )) are ϵ -interleaved.


It is now clear that an equivalent definition of the bottleneck distance between bipath modules M and N can be given by

$$
d _ { B } ( M , N ) \coloneqq \inf \{ \epsilon \, | \, \text {there is an $\epsilon$-matching between $\mathcal{Ob}(M)$ and $\mathcal{Ob}(N)$} \} ,
$$

and such the ϵ -matchings need to match intervals of the same type in the bipath poset, as in the situation for block-decomposable modules of Lemma 3.3.

# 5. Fibered bar and arc codes

Lesnick and Wright [22] introduced the fibered barcode , an invariant of persistence modules over R 2 given by the collection of barcodes of all 1-D affine slices. In detail, denoting by L the collection of all (unparametrized) lines in R 2 with non-negative slope and for a fixed persistence module M : R 2 → Vec , each line l ∈ L induces a single-parameter persistence module M | l = M ◦ i L along the inclusion i L : l   → R 2 . The fibered barcode of M is then the assignment of each line l ∈ L to the barcode of M | l .

Closely following the construction of the fibered barcode, and due to bipath modules having arc codes, given a bipath poset B and a persistence module M : R 2 → Vec , an order-preserving map f : B → R 2 induces a bipath module M | f = M ◦ f and we can define the fibered arc code :

Definition 5.1. The fibered arc code of a persistence module M : R 2 → Vec is the assignment of each order-preserving map f : B → R 2 , where B is a bipath poset, to the arc code of M | f .

As already known, the fibered barcode is an incomplete invariant. The same applies to the fibered arc code. Still, the fibered arc code gives more information than the fibered barcode: in Example 5.2 below we show two non-isomorphic persistence modules with equal fibered barcode, but different fibered arc code. In addition, in Example 5.3 we show two non-isomorphic persistence modules with equal fibered arc code. To distinguish the modules in this last example, one would need to use different invariants like the interval rank invariant , proposed by Asashiba, Gauthier and Liu [7], or other related invariants like the generalized rank invariant , proposed by Kim and Mémoli [21].
