# NERVE MODELS OF SUBDIVISION BIFILTRATIONS

# MICHAEL LESNICK AND KEN MCCABE

Abstract. We study the size of Sheehy’s subdivision bifiltrations, up to homotopy. We focus in particular on the subdivision-Rips bifiltration SR ( X ) of a metric space X , the only density-sensitive bifiltration on metric spaces known to satisfy a strong robustness property. Given a simplicial filtration F with a total of m maximal simplices across all indices, we introduce a nerve-based simplicial model for its subdivision bifiltration SF whose k -skeleton has size O ( m k +1 ) . We also show that the 0 -skeleton of any simplicial model of SF has size at least m . We give several applications: For an arbitrary metric space X , we introduce a √ 2 -approximation to SR ( X ) , denoted J ( X ) , whose k -skeleton has size O ( | X | k +2 ) . This improves on the previous best approximation bound of √ 3 , achieved by the degree-Rips bifiltration, which implies that J ( X ) is more robust than degree-Rips. Moreover, we show that the approximation factor of √ 2 is tight; in particular, there exists no exact model of SR ( X ) with poly-size skeleta. On the other hand, we show that for X in a fixed-dimensional Euclidean space with the ℓ p -metric, there exists an exact model of SR ( X ) with poly-size skeleta for p ∈ { 1 , ∞} , as well as a (1 + ϵ ) -approximation to SR ( X ) with poly-size skeleta for any p ∈ (1 , ∞ ) and fixed ϵ > 0 .

# 1. Introduction

1.1. Background. Topological data analysis (TDA) [21, 28, 52, 82] studies the shape of a data set by constructing a diagram of topological spaces and computing invariants of this diagram. In particular, persistent homology [39, 46, 67], the mostly widely studied and applied TDA technique, begins by constructing a 1-parameter filtration of spaces, e.g., the Čech filtration of a Euclidean point cloud or the (Vietoris-)Rips filtration of a finite metric space [38]. Applying the homology functor with field coefficients to a filtration yields a diagram of vector spaces called a persistence module, whose isomorphism type is fully described by a multiset of intervals called a barcode .

The barcodes of Čech and Rips filtrations are well-known to be stable with respect to small perturbations of the data [27, 29, 31]. However, these barcodes can be highly unstable with respect to outliers, and also insensitive to variations in density [12, Section 4 ]. Several strategies have been proposed to address these limitations within the framework of 1-parameter persistence; see [13, Section 1.7]. However, as noted in [13], these strategies share a common disadvantage, which is that they depend on a choice of one or more parameters. It can be unclear how to best choose these parameters, and in some settings, it can be that no single choice allows us to fully capture the topological structure of interest in our data. This motivates us to analyze the topology of point cloud or metric data by constructing a bifiltration , treating density and spatial scale as separate parameters [22].

The branch of TDA that studies data via the homology of multiparameter filtrations is known as multiparameter persistent homology (MPH) [17, 22]. Though there are difficulties with extending the notion of a barcode to the multiparameter setting, several simple, albeit incomplete, invariants of multiparameter persistence modules have been proposed and studied. These can be used as surrogates for a barcode in applications; see [17, Sections 4 and 9] for an overview.

The problem of working with 2-parameter persistent homology in a practical, computationally efficient way has been actively studied in recent years, leading to significant advances in algorithms and software [3, 4, 7, 23, 45, 56– 58, 70, 79, 86], as well as several interesting applications [1, 8, 30, 34, 58, 80,

[Page 3]

85]. We expect such applications will become more common as multiparameter persistence software continues to improve and is more fully integrated into standard ecosystems for TDA and machine learning. However, the applicability of some of the prevailing 2-parameter approaches is limited by relatively high computational cost, and much current research in this area centers on limiting this cost.

1.2. Density-Sensitive Bifiltrations. There are multiple ways of constructing a density-sensitive bifiltration from point cloud or metric data, which vary in their robustness to outliers, computability, and generality [12]. We now review several of these constructions, focusing on those that do not depend on an additional parameter choice; see [3, 10, 22, 26] for other constructions.

We denote the Rips filtration of a finite metric space X as R ( X ) . For X equipped with an embedding into an ambient metric space Y (e.g., Y = R n ), we denote the Čech filtration of X as ˇ C ( X ) , suppressing the dependence on Y in the notation; see Section 2.1.1 for the definitions. Sheehy [74] introduced the subdivision-Rips and subdivision-Čech bifiltrations SR ( X ) and S ˇ C ( X ) , which refine R ( X ) and ˇ C ( X ) , respectively. In fact, Sheehy’s construction provides a bifiltered refinement SF of any simplicial filtration F . The definition of SF amounts to the observation that there is a natural filtration on the barycentric subdivision of any simplicial complex. Unfortunately, the sizes of both SR ( X ) and S ˇ C ( X ) are exponential in | X | , so in practice we cannot directly compute them.

Sheehy, Cavanna, and Gardners’ multicover nerve theorem [13, 24, 74], a 2-parameter extension of the standard topological equivalence between the Čech and union-of-balls filtrations, motivates and illuminates the subdivisionČech bifiltration. The theorem says that if finite intersections of balls are contractible in the ambient metric space Y , then S ˇ C ( X ) is homotopically equivalent (i.e., weakly equivalent , see Section 2.1) to the multicover bifiltration M ( X ) . The bifiltration M ( X ) is an extension of the union-of-balls filtration, where one filters both by radius and by cover multiplicity , i.e., the minimum number of balls covering a point. d

[Page 4]

Lesnick and Wright [56] introduced another simple density-sensitive bifiltration on a finite metric space X , the degree-Rips bifiltration DR ( X ) , whose k -skeleton has size at most O ( | X | k +2 ) . Restricting DR ( X ) to a grid of constant size yields a bifiltration whose skeleta have size O ( | X | k +1 ) , which asymptotically matches the size of the 1-parameter Rips filtration. This is small enough that H 0 ( DR ( X )) and invariants thereof can readily be computed for real data sets with thousands of points. The software packages RIVET [77] and Persistable [73] support such computations.

Furthermore, Blumberg and Lesnick [13] showed that S ˇ C ( − ) and SR ( − ) both satisfy strong robustness properties, closely paralleling the standard stability theorems of single-parameter persistence. In the case of SR ( − ) , the result is the following:

Theorem 1.1 ([13, Theorem 1.6(iii)]) . For any finite, non-empty metric spaces X and X ′ , the homotopy interleaving distance between SR ( X ) and SR ( X ′ ) is at most the Gromov-Prohorov distance between the uniform probability measures on X and X ′ .

In addition, [13, Theorem 1 . 7 (ii)] gives a robustness result for DR ( − ) analogous to Theorem 1.1, formulated in terms of affine homotopy interleavings , where the shifts between indices in the interleaving are affine maps rather than translations. This a far weaker robustness property than that of SR ( − ) , but is tight, in a sense.

Rolle and Scoccola [72] give a Lipschitz stability result for DR ( − ) with respect to the Gromov-Hausdorff-Prohorov distance . However, since that distance is not robust to outliers, Rolle and Scoccola’s result does not speak to the robustness of DR ( − ) .

1.3. Contributions. Together, the above results reveal a tension between between robustness, computability, and generality for density-sensitive bifiltrations: The more general and more robust a bifiltration is, the larger and less computable it is, at least with current technology. Ideally, one would like a bifiltration that is robust (say, in the sense of Theorem 1.1), computable, and defined for arbitrary finite metric spaces. No such construction is known at present, and the problem of understanding what can and cannot be done in this direction is largely open.

# 2. Preliminaries

We assume familiarity with basic algebraic topology, graph theory, and category theory, as introduced, e.g., in [15, 50, 69]. For additional background on TDA and persistent homology, see, e.g., [14, 20, 21, 36, 38, 64].

Let P be a poset, which we regard as a category in the usual way, i.e. the object set is P and morphisms are pairs p ≤ q . For any category C , functor F : P → C , and p ≤ q in P , we write F ( p ) as F p and F ( p ≤ q ): F ( p ) → F ( q )

[Page 8]

as F p → q . We call the morphisms F p → q structure maps . The functors P → C form a category C P , whose morphisms are the natural transformations.

Given posets P 1 ,...,P l , we regard the Cartesian product P 1 ×···× P n as a poset, where ( p 1 ,...p n ) ≤ ( q 1 ,...,q n ) if and only if p i ≤ q i for each i .

2.1. Filtrations. A ( P -)filtration is a functor F : P → Top such that the map F p → q is a subspace inclusion for all p ≤ q in P . If F is Simp -valued, we call F a simplicial filtration. When P is a totally ordered set, we call F a 1-parameter filtration. If P = T 1 × T 2 for totally ordered sets T 1 and T 2 , we call F : P → Top a bifiltration .

We will also consider functors F : P → Top that are not filtrations, particularly those of the following form: For P = T 1 × T 2 with each T i totally ordered, we call F : P → Top a semifiltration if for each fixed t ∈ T 2 , the restriction F − ,t : T 1 × { t } → Top is a filtration.

2.1.1. Clique and Čech Filtrations. A graph is a simplicial complex of dimension at most one. A graph filtration is a graph-valued filtration G : P → Simp . Any such graph filtration induces a simplicial filtration G : P → Simp , called the clique filtration of G , where each G p is the clique complex of G p , i.e., G p is the largest simplicial complex with 1-skeleton G p . For a key example, let X be a metric space with metric d X and let r ≥ 0 . The r -neighborhood graph G ( X ) r is the graph with vertex set X and an edge [ x,y ] if and only d X ( x,y ) ≤ 2 r . The graphs G ( X ) r assemble into a graph filtration G ( X ) : [0 , ∞ ) → Simp . We call R ( X ) : = G ( X ) the (Vietoris)-Rips filtration of X .

# 6. Computation

We now briefly discuss computation. Specifically, we address the problem of computing the fixed-dimensional skeleta of the nerve-based semifiltrations appearing in the statements of our main theorems. We concern ourselves only with the modest goal of computing these skeleta in time polynomial in

[Page 28]

the total size of input and output (i.e., output-sensitive polynomial time ), leaving a more nuanced study of computation to future work.

Observe that for a finitely presented filtration F : N → Simp , to compute the 0 -skeleton of NF it suffices to compute the set Σ t of maximal simplices of each F t . Once we have done so, to compute the k -skeleton, it suffices to test all j -sets of each Σ t for non-empty intersections, where j ≤ k + 1 . For fixed k , this can be done naively in polynomial time.

When F is the clique filtration of a graph filtration G (e.g., a Rips filtration), we can compute the maximal simplices of F t in output-sensitive polynomial time using well-known algorithms for enumerating all maximal cliques in a graph [25, 59, 78].

To compute the 0-skeleton of the semifiltration J ( X ) of Corollary 1.5, it suffices to compute the maximal simplices of the intrinsic Čech filtration I ( X ) . As these maximal simplices are metric balls (see the proof of Lemma 4.1), we can compute them naively in polynomial time by computing all metric balls at each index and doing containment tests to determine which balls are maximal.

To compute a semifiltration as in the statement of Corollary 1.8(ii) in polynomial time (for fixed d and ϵ ), we adapt the construction underlying the proof of Corollary 1.8(ii). The main task is to compute the filtered intersection graph P ( X ) . We preface our discussion of this with a technical caveat: Whereas each vertex of P ( X ) r is a polytope with at most f ( √ d/ϵ ) facets, our algorithm will produce a variant of P ( X ) r for which this number of facets is most f (4 √ d/ϵ ) . Appendix A. Constructing a Bifiltration from a Semifiltration

The simplicial models in this paper are generally not bifiltrations, but rather semifiltrations. However, computation of 2-parameter persistent homology is simplest and most closely aligned with existing software [7, 45, 56, 57] when the input is bifiltered. Therefore, we wish to understand how to efficiently convert a semifiltration into a weakly equivalent bifiltration.

Extending work of Dey et al. [35], Kerber and Schreiber [53] have given a nice solution to the 1-parameter version of this problem. Namely, [53] gives a construction to transform a finitely presented functor F : N → Simp into a weakly equivalent filtration F ′ such that, letting | · | denote size, we have

$$
| \mathcal { F } ^ { \prime } | = O ( \Delta | \mathcal { F } | \log | \mathcal { F } ^ { 0 } | ) ,
$$

where ∆ = max t dim( F t ) and F 0 is the 0-skeleton of F . The authors also give an efficient algorithm for computing F ′ .

The approach of [53] extends to convert a finitely presented simplicial semifiltration A : N op × N → Simp to a weakly equivalent bifiltration A ′ , with the same asymptotic increase in size. This amounts to applying the construction of [53] to the functor A 1 , − : N → Simp , yielding a filtration A ′ 1 , − that inherits a bifiltered structure from A .

To explain this more fully, we recall some details of the construction of [53]. By factoring the structure maps of F : N → Simp , we may assume without loss of generality that each structure map F t → t +1 is either is an identity map, an inclusion inserting a single simplex, or a vertex collapse , i.e., a surjection that maps exactly two vertices { u,v } to a single vertex w . If we choose the factorization of each structure map to have a minimal number of factors, then the factorization does not change the size of F .

The construction of F ′ is inductive in t . We maintain a label for each vertex in F ′ t , marking the vertex as active or inactive . If F t → t +1 is an identity map, then we also take F ′ t → t +1 to be an identity. If F t → t +1 inserts a new simplex σ , then we take F ′ t +1 = F ′ t ∪ { σ } , and if σ is a vertex, then we also mark it as active in F ′ t +1 . Now suppose F t → t +1 is vertex collapse mapping

[Page 30]

{ u,v } to w . To construct F ′ t +1 , we choose one of the vertices { u,v } . Let V be the subcomplex of the closed star 1 of v in F ′ t spanned by active vertices, and let X [ t, F ] be the simplicial cone on V with apex u . We take F ′ t +1 = F ′ t ∪ X [ t, F ] . The vertex v is then marked as inactive, and each subsequent instance of w in F is renamed u . See [53, Figure 1] for an illustration. Either choice of u or v leads to a topologically correct construction, so [53] makes the choice in a way that minimizes the size of the resulting filtration. However, the asymptotic bound (A.1) on |F ′ | requires the choice of active vertex to be the one that leads to the smaller choice of F ′ t +1 . Henceforth, we assume without loss of generality that every vertex collapse we encounter is of the form { u,v }  → u , where v is subsequently marked inactive.

Next, we consider how to extend this approach to convert a finitely presented simplicial semifiltration A to a bifiltration A ′ . Without loss of generality, we assume as above that the structure maps A (1 ,t ) → (1 ,t +1) are either identities, inclusions of single simplices, or vertex collapses. We apply the 1-parameter construction to A 1 , − , yielding a filtration A ′ 1 , − . Then, to define A ′ , for ( k,t ) ∈ N op × N , we take A ′ k,t to be the maximal subcomplex of A ′ 1 ,t on the vertices   t ′ ≤ t A 0 k,t ′ , where A 0 denotes the 0-skeleton of A . Informally, then, A ′ k, − is the filtration obtained by restricting of construction of A ′ 1 , − to the subfunctor A k, − ⊂ A 1 , − .

# Proposition A.1. The bifiltration A ′ is weakly equivalent to A .

Proof. We have a natural simplicial epimorphism ρ : A ′ → A defined as follows: For ( k,t ) ∈ N op × N , a vertex x ∈ A ′ k,t , and s ≤ t such that x ∈ A k,s , we let ρ k,t ( x ) = A ( k,s ) → ( k,t ) ( x ) . One easily checks that this definition does not depend on the choice of s . For fixed ( k,t ) , let S ⊂ N denote the set of indices s < t such that A ( k,s ) → ( k,s +1) is a collapse. The map ρ k,t factors as a sequence of collapses of the simplicial cones { X [ s, A k, − ] | s ∈ S } , where the collapses are done in order of increasing s . Specifically, if A ( k,s ) → ( k,s +1) is the collapse { u,v }  → u , then we collapse the cone X [ s, A k, − ] onto the image of the simplicial endomorphism of X [ s, A k, − ] mapping v to u and every other vertex to itself. By Quillen’s Theorem A for simplicial complexes [5], or by a simple discrete Morse theory argument, each cone collapse is a homotopy equivalence, and so the map ρ k,t is a homotopy equivalence. Hence ρ is an objectwise homotopy equivalence. □
