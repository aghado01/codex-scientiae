## Abstract

Distributed principal component analysis (PCA) produces node-level estimates of both a mean vector and a principal subspace. Robustly aggregating these heterogeneous objects requires a relative scale between mean error and subspace error. We study a scale-calibrated median-of-means estimator for this problem using the product geometry of Euclidean space and the Grassmann manifold. A node-level PCA expansion shows that the mean component has the usual linear influence, whereas the subspace component is an eigengap-weighted covariance perturbation. We prove a local reduction showing that the proposed product-manifold medianof-means estimator is asymptotically equivalent to a scaled spatial median of node influence errors. This yields fixed-node non-Gaussian limits, growing-node Gaussian limits with finite-block bias, and an explicit scale-dependent covariance formula. We propose robust block-scale and inference-optimal calibration rules, establish high-probability median-of-means bounds, characterize factorwise bad-node influence, and prove node-bootstrap validity. Simulations and largescale single-cell RNA-seq data show that scale calibration adapts to eigengap-driven subspace uncertainty and provides a robust distributed PCA summary.

## 1 Introduction

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a basic tool for summarizing high-dimensional data by a lowdimensional mean–subspace representation. Since the classical formulations of Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933), PCA has been used both as a dimension-reduction method and as an inferential summary of dominant variation. Its fixed-dimensional asymptotics and eigenspace perturbation behavior are well understood (Anderson; 1963; Davis and Kahan; 1970; Stewart and Sun; 1990; Kat¯; 1995). These properties make PCA a natural target for distributed computation: each node can compute a local PCA summary, and only the local summaries need to be transmitted.

A local PCA summary, however, is not a single Euclidean object. It consists of a mean vector and a principal subspace. The mean lies in $R^p$ , whereas the leading $r$ -dimensional subspace lies on the Grassmann manifold $\mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$ (Edelman et al.; 1998). We use the Grassmann formulation because PCA identifies a subspace, not a particular signed, ordered, or oriented orthonormal basis. A Stiefel representation would introduce artificial orientation conventions and becomes unstable when eigenvalues inside the leading block are tied or nearly tied.

The statistical stability of the two components can be very different. A node mean is governed by first-order variation of X , whereas a node eigenspace is governed by eigenprojector perturbation and is strongly affected by eigengaps. When the leading eigenspace is weakly separated, the subspace component can be much noisier than the mean component. This imbalance is further amplified under heavy tails or corrupted nodes.

Our goal is to robustly aggregate node-level PCA estimates

$$
\widehat{\theta}_{k} = ( \widehat{\mu}_{k} , \widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{k} ) \in R^{p} \times \text{Gr} ( r , p ) .
$$

Median-of-means aggregation is a natural way to combine robustness and scalability (Nemirovskij et al.; 1983; Jerrum et al.; 1986; Alon et al.; 1999; Minsker; 2015; Lugosi and Mendelson; 2019). In the present problem, however, the median must respect the product geometry of a Euclidean mean and a Grassmann subspace. This leads to the scale-calibrated product MoM estimator studied below.

Once PCA is viewed this way, a scale issue becomes unavoidable. Let $B_{1}$ ,...,B K be nodes of size b , with n = Kb , and let $\theta_k$ = ( $\mu_k$ , U k ) be the local PCA estimate. We study the scaled geometric MoM estimator

$$
\widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} = \underset{( \mu , \mu ) \in R^{p \times G r ( r , p )}}{\arg \min} \, \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} \left \{\alpha \| \mu - \widehat{\mu}_{k} \|^{2} + ( 2 - \alpha ) d_{G r} ( \mathcal{U} , \widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{k} )^{2} \right \}^{1 / 2} ,
$$

where $d_{\mathrm{Gr}}$ is the canonical Grassmann distance and $\alpha \in (0,2)$. The scale $\alpha$ balances Euclidean mean error against subspace error, which determines how much a node must disagree in its mean or eigenspace before it is treated as unreliable by the final aggregation.

This paper studies how α should be chosen and how it changes inference in robust distributed PCA. The question is related to, but distinct from, ordinary scale selection for product-manifold medians. For ordinary product medians, changing factor weights can change the population center because the objective couples factors through a common radial distance; fixed-scale existence, robustness, limit theory, bootstrap validity, and algorithms are available from Park and You (2026). Here, the aggregated objects are node estimators, so scale acts on node-level errors. In centered MoM regimes, the target may remain ( $\mu_0$ , $U_0$ ), but $\alpha_c$hanges covariance, finite-block bias, concentration, confidence regions, and bad-node influence. Scale calibration is therefore an inferential design problem: small eigengaps call for downweighting noisy Grassmann errors, whereas unstable mean estimation calls for downweighting Euclidean errors. Our contributions are as follows.

1. We formulate robust distributed PCA as MoM estimation on $R^p \times \mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$ and derive the joint influence expansion of ($\hat{\mu}$ k ,$\hat{U}$ k ). The mean influence is X -$\mu_0$ and the Grassmann influence is an eigengap-weighted covariance perturbation.

- 2. We prove that the product-manifold MoM estimator is asymptotically equivalent to a scaled spatial median of node influence errors. This yields both fixedK non-Gaussian and growingK Gaussian limits with finite-block bias.
- 3. We derive the scale-dependent covariance of the centered aggregate. If $H_\alpha$ is the block scale matrix and W is the limiting node influence error, then

$$
V_{\alpha} = H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} A_{\alpha}^{- 1} S_{\alpha} A_{\alpha}^{- T} H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} ,
$$

where $A_\alpha$ and $S_\alpha$ are the derivative and score covariance of the spatial median of $H_{1}$ / 2 α W . This leads to robust block-scale and inference-optimal calibration rules.

4. We establish high-probability MoM bounds, factorwise mean–subspace deviation tradeoffs, bad-node robustness, scale-dependent influence constants α − 1 / 2 and (2 − α ) − 1 / 2 , and nodebootstrap validity.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the mean–subspace PCA parameter space $R^p \times \mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$, defines the scaled product metric, and states the standing assumptions. Section 3 derives the node-level PCA influence expansion and proves the local reduction from product-manifold MoM aggregation to a scaled spatial median of node errors. Section 4 develops the two asymptotic regimes: a fixedK non-Gaussian limit and a growingK Gaussian limit with finite-block bias. Section 5 studies scale-dependent inference, including the covariance formula, robust block-scale calibration, inference-optimal scale selection, and the effect of estimating $\alpha$ . Section 6 gives finite-sample concentration, bad-node robustness, factorwise influence bounds, and node bootstrap validity. Section 7 presents simulations and a large-scale single-cell RNA-seq analysis. Section 8 concludes with implications and open directions. Additional technical results, all proofs, and supplementary numerical experiments are provided in the Appendix.

## 2 Robust distributed PCA on R p × Gr( r,p )

We begin with the statistical object that motivates the paper. We note that while we study PCA, the later local MoM theory applies to more general product-valued estimators once a node-level asymptotic expansion is available.

### 2.1 PCA target and Grassmann geometry

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ ∈ $R^p$ be independent copies of a random vector X . Write

$$
\mu_{0} = E [ X ] , \quad \Sigma = E [ ( X - \mu_{0} ) ( X - \mu_{0} )^{T} ] .
$$

Denote the eigendecomposition of Σ by

$$
\Sigma = \sum_{j = 1}^{p} \lambda_{j} u_{j} u_{j}^{T} , \quad \lambda_{1} \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_{p} ,
$$

with an eigengap

$$
\lambda_{r} > \lambda_{r + 1} .
$$

The leading $r$ -dimensional principal subspace is

$$
\mathcal{U}_{0} = \text{span} ( u_{1} , \dots , u_{r} ) \in \text{Gr} ( r , p ) .
$$

The parameter of interest is

$$
\theta_{0} = ( \mu_{0} , \mathcal{U}_{0} ) \in \mathcal{M}_{p} \colon = R^{p} \times \text{Gr} ( r , p ) .
$$

The Grassmann distance is defined by principal angles. If $\vartheta_1, \ldots, \vartheta_r$ ∈ [0 ,π/ 2] are the principal angles between U and V , then r

$$
d_{\text{Gr}} ( \mathcal{U} , \mathcal{V} )^{2} = \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \vartheta_{j}^{2} .
$$

This distance is invariant under the choice of orthonormal bases. After choosing an eigenbasis $U_r =$ ( $u_{1}$ ,...,u r ) and an orthogonal complement $U^\perp$ = ( u r +1 ,...,u p ), the tangent space T $U_0$ $\mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$ is identified with matrices in $R^{(p-r) \times r}$ , equipped with the Frobenius norm.

For $\alpha \in (0,2)$, define the scaled product distance

$$
d_{\alpha} \{( \mu , \mathcal{U} ) , ( \mu^{\prime} , \mathcal{V} ) \}^{2} = \alpha \| \mu - \mu^{\prime} \|^{2} + ( 2 - \alpha ) d_{\text{Gr}} ( \mathcal{U} , \mathcal{V} )^{2} .
$$

We will often restrict α to a compact interval

$$
I_{\varepsilon} = [ \varepsilon , 2 - \varepsilon ] , \quad 0 < \varepsilon < 1 .
$$

This is not merely technical. As later robustness bounds show, the factorwise influence constants diverge as $\alpha \downarrow 0$ or $\alpha \uparrow 2$.

### 2.2 Local estimators and median aggregation

For simplicity, we assume that { 1 ,...,n } can be partitioned into K disjoint nodes $B_{1}$ ,...,B K of equal size b , with n = Kb . On node $B_k$ , define

$$
\widehat{\mu}_{k} = \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} X_{i} ,
$$

$$
\widehat{\Sigma}_{k} = \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} ( X_{i} - \widehat{\mu}_{k} ) ( X_{i} - \widehat{\mu}_{k} )^{T} ,
$$

and let U k ∈ $\mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$ be the leading $r$ -dimensional eigenspace of $\Sigma_k$ . The local estimator is $\theta_k$ = ( $\mu_k$ , U k ), and the scaleα geometric MoM estimator is

$$
\widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} = \arg \min_{\theta \in \mathcal{M}_{p}} Q_{K , \alpha} ( \theta ) , \quad Q_{K , \alpha} ( \theta ) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} d_{\alpha} ( \theta , \widehat{\theta}_{k} ) .
$$

For fixed $\alpha$ , this is a geometric median of points on a product manifold. The general productmanifold theory therefore supplies the fixed-scale existence, local uniqueness, robustness, central limit, bootstrap, and computational foundations (Park and You; 2026). This paper does not revisit those fixed-scale product-median facts. Instead, it studies how the scale $\alpha$ interacts with the nodelevel PCA error distribution.

### 2.3 Tangent notation and assumptions

Let d = p + r ( p − r ) be the dimension of the product tangent space. In tangent coordinates at $\theta_0$ = ( $\mu_0$ , $U_0$ ), define

$$
H_{\alpha} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha I_{p} & 0 \\ 0 & ( 2 - \alpha ) I_{r ( p - r )} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

For w = ( $w_\mu$ ,$w_U$ ) ∈ $R^p \oplus T$ $U_0$ $\mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$, write

$$
\| w \|_{H_{\alpha}}^{2} = w^{T} H_{\alpha} w = \alpha \| w_{\mu} \|^{2} + ( 2 - \alpha ) \| w_{\mathcal{U}} \|^{2} .
$$

We use the following assumptions throughout the theoretical development. The first two conditions ensure that the node PCA estimators are well-defined in a common local coordinate system. The third condition is the usual regularity condition for spatial-median asymptotics applied to the node-level errors. The final condition is only needed for refined growingK inference with finite-block bias.

### (A1) Moments and eigengap. The observations satisfy

$$
E \| X - \mu_{0} \|^{4 + \delta} < \infty
$$

for some δ > 0, and the population covariance matrix satisfies the eigengap condition (3). The eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ may have multiplicities within the leading block and within the trailing block. Only the separation between the leading $r$ -dimensional eigenspace and its orthogonal complement is required.

(A2) Node localization. There is a normal neighborhood N of $U_0$ in $\mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$ such that max $1 \le k \le K$ 1 { U k / ∈

N} → 0 in probability. For fixed K , this follows from U k → $U_0$ in probability. For growing K , it is enough to assume

$$
K \, P ( \dot{\mathcal{U}}_{1} \notin \mathcal{N} ) \rightarrow 0 .
$$

The same neighborhood is used for all α ∈ I ε .

(A3) Spatial median regularity. Let

$$
W_{k , b} = \sqrt{b} \begin{pmatrix} \widehat{\mu}_{k} - \mu_{0} \\ \log_{\mathcal{U}_{0}} ( \widehat{\mathcal{U}}_{k} ) \end{pmatrix}
$$

be the node error in product tangent coordinates, and let $W_b$ denote a generic copy. For each α ∈ I ε , define

$$
s_{\alpha , b} = \arg \min_{s \in R^{d}} E \| s - W_{b} \|_{H_{\alpha}} .
$$

The minimizer is unique. With

$$
Y_{\alpha , b} = H_{\alpha}^{1 / 2} ( W_{b} - s_{\alpha , b} ) , \ \ R_{\alpha , b} = \| Y_{\alpha , b} \| , \ \ U_{\alpha , b} = Y_{\alpha , b} / R_{\alpha , b} ,
$$

we assume P ( R α,b = 0) = 0, E R − 1 α,b < ∞ , and

$$
A_{\alpha , b} = E \left [ \frac{I - U_{\alpha , b} U_{\alpha , b}^{T}}{R_{\alpha , b}} \right ]
$$

is nonsingular uniformly over α ∈ I ε .

(A4) Finite-block bias expansion. For refined growingK inference, the finite-block median satisfies

$$
s_{\alpha , b} = b^{- 1 / 2} a_{\alpha} + o ( b^{- 1 / 2} )
$$

uniformly over α ∈ I ε , for a continuous function α → a α .

The finite-block bias s α,b is the population spatial median of the finite-sample node error distribution. It vanishes under central symmetry, but otherwise can be of order b − 1 / 2 . Hence it may survive rootn scaling unless K/b → 0.

## 3 Local PCA asymptotics and MoM reductions

In this section, we convert robust distributed PCA into a problem about spatial medians in a Euclidean tangent space. This reduction allows the later covariance and robustness results to be expressed in explicit forms. It proceeds in two steps. First, we linearize the node PCA estimator. Second, we show that the geometric median of node estimators is locally the spatial median of the linearized errors.

### 3.1 Influence expansion for node PCA

Let

$$
E_{X} = ( X - \mu_{0} ) ( X - \mu_{0} )^{T} - \Sigma .
$$

Choose the eigenbasis $u_{1}$ ,...,u p used in the decomposition of Σ. For a = 1 ,...,p − r and j = 1 ,...,r , define T

$$
B ( X )_{a j} = \frac{u_{r + a}^{T} E_{X} u_{j}}{\lambda_{j} - \lambda_{r + a}} .
$$

This matrix is the first-order tangent representation of the leading subspace perturbation. The denominators are eigengaps between the leading eigenspace and its complement. Thus the formula already reveals the statistical difficulty of PCA such that subspace uncertainty is amplified when the leading and trailing eigenvalues are close.

Theorem 1 (Node PCA influence expansion) . Suppose Assumption (A1) holds. For a node $B_k$ , define W k,b as in (11) . Then

$$
W_{k , b} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{b}} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} \zeta ( X_{i} ) + r_{k , b} , \quad r_{k , b} \stackrel{p}{\rightarrow} 0 ,
$$

where

Consequently,

$$
\zeta ( X ) = \left ( \begin{matrix} X - \mu_{0} \\ \text{sec} \end{matrix} \right ) \in R^{p + r ( p - r )} .
$$

$$
W_{k , b} \sim W \sim \mathcal{N} ( 0 , \Gamma ) , \quad \Gamma = V a r \{\zeta ( X ) \} .
$$

If K = $K_{n}$ → ∞ , the expansion is uniform over nodes whenever

$$
\sqrt{K} \max_{1 \leq k \leq K} \| r_{k , b} \| \stackrel{p}{\rightarrow} 0 .
$$

The theorem is a first-order spectral perturbation expansion written in product tangent coordinates. The mean part is linear, while the Grassmann part is an eigengap-weighted covariance perturbation. Thus the covariance Γ contains mean variability, subspace variability, and their crosscovariance, which are precisely the quantities later affected by scale.

### 3.2 Local reduction to a scaled spatial median

The next result explains why the product-manifold aggregation can be studied through the spatial median of W k,b . Define

$$
\widehat{s}_{K , b , \alpha} = \arg \min_{s \in R^{d}} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} \| s - W_{k , b} \|_{H_{\alpha}} .
$$

This is the scaled spatial median of the node PCA errors in tangent coordinates, which corresponds to the first-order representation of the actual geometric MoM estimator.

Theorem 2 (Local reduction) . Suppose Assumptions (A1), (A2), and (A3) hold. If K is fixed, then uniformly over α ∈ I ε , √

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Xi \, I_{\varepsilon} , \\ \sqrt{b} \log_{\theta_{0}} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} = \widehat{s}_{K , b , \alpha} + o_{p} ( 1 ) .
\end{aligned}
$$

If K → ∞ , the stronger relation

$$
\sqrt{K} \sup_{\alpha \in I_{\varepsilon}} \left \| \sqrt{b} \log_{\theta_{0}} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} - \widehat{s}_{K , b , \alpha} \right \| \stackrel{p}{\rightarrow} 0
$$

holds provided the local Riemannian objective approximation error is o p ( K − 1 / 2 ) uniformly over α ∈ I ε . A sufficient condition is √ K/b → 0 together with a uniformly bounded third moment for the localized node errors.

The theorem reduces the nonlinear product-manifold aggregation problem to an ordinary spatial median problem in $R^d$ . All scale-dependent covariance and robustness calculations below are consequences of this tangent-space representation.

## 4 Limit theory for scale-calibrated MoM PCA

The local reduction separates two sources of asymptotics: the within-node limit that creates the error distribution W , and the across-node median that aggregates K errors. These two sources lead to two regimes. If K is fixed, the limit is a finite geometric median of random vectors and is generally non-Gaussian. If K → ∞ , the spatial median itself has a central limit theorem, but the finite-block population median s α,b can contribute a bias term.

### 4.1 Fixed number of nodes

Theorem 3 (FixedK limit) . Suppose K is fixed and the conditions of Theorems 1 and 2 hold. Let $W_{1}$ ,...,W K be independent copies of W ∼ N (0 , Γ) . If

$$
T_{\alpha , K} = \arg \min_{s \in R^{d}} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} \| s - W_{k} \|_{H^{\alpha}}
$$

is unique almost surely, then

Equivalently,

$$
\sqrt{b} \log_{\theta_{n} , \alpha} \sim T_{\alpha , K} .
$$

$$
\sqrt{n} \log_{\theta_{n} , \alpha} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} \sim \sqrt{K} \, T_{\alpha , K} .
$$

This regime is relevant when the number of machines is fixed. The estimator is rootn , but the

limit is generally non-Gaussian because it is the spatial median of finitely many Gaussian node errors.

### 4.2 Growing number of nodes

When K → ∞ , the spatial median of the node errors has its own central limit theorem. The centering, however, is the finite-block population median s α,b , not necessarily zero. This distinction is essential as MoM has two asymptotic levels, and the finite-block asymmetry of the node estimator can survive rootn scaling if K grows too quickly relative to b .

Define

$$
S_{\alpha , b} = V a r ( U_{\alpha , b} ) ,
$$

where U α,b was defined in Assumption (A3). The covariance associated with the spatial median of finite-block errors is

$$
V_{\alpha , b} = H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} A_{\alpha , b}^{- 1} S_{\alpha , b} A_{\alpha , b}^{- T} H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} .
$$

Theorem 4 (GrowingK CLT with finite-block bias) . Suppose K → ∞ , b → ∞ , and n = Kb . Under Assumptions (A1)–(A3) and the growingK reduction condition in (22) ,

$$
\sqrt{K} \left ( \widehat{s}_{K , b , \alpha} - s_{\alpha , b} \right ) \sim \mathcal{N} ( 0 , V_{\alpha , b} ) .
$$

Consequently,

$$
\sqrt{n} \log_{\theta_{0}} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} = \sqrt{K} \, s_{\alpha , b} + Z_{\alpha , b} + o_{p} ( 1 ) ,
$$

where Z α,b ⇝ N (0 ,V α ) whenever V α,b → V α . If √ K s α,b → δ α , then

$$
\sqrt{n} \log_{\theta_{0}} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} \sim \mathcal{N} ( \delta_{\alpha} , V_{\alpha} ) .
$$

In particular, if √ K s α,b → 0 , then the limit is centered.

Corollary 5 (Symmetry and the node growth condition) . If the finite-block node error distribution is centrally symmetric, $W_b$ d = − $W_b$ , then s α,b = 0 for every α ∈ I ε , and the centered CLT holds. More generally, if Assumption (A4) holds, then

$$
\sqrt{K} \, s_{\alpha , b} = \sqrt{K / b} \, a_{\alpha} + o ( \sqrt{K / b} ) .
$$

Thus, absent symmetry or cancellation of a α , centered rootn inference requires

$$
K / b \rightarrow 0 .
$$

Corollary 5 gives a concrete design lesson. Increasing K improves robustness to bad nodes and parallel scalability, but it reduces the node size b . If the node PCA error has finite-block skewness, the product median aggregation can have a rootn centering bias unless the nodes remain large

## 5 Scale-dependent inference and calibration

We now turn from limit laws to scale selection. The first result gives the limiting covariance as a function of α . The remaining results use that covariance to justify block-noise calibration, robust empirical scale estimation, and inference-optimal tuning.

### 5.1 The covariance induced by a scale

Assume the limiting node error is W ∼ N (0 , Γ). For α ∈ I ε , define

$$
Y_{\alpha} = H_{\alpha}^{1 / 2} W , \quad R_{\alpha} = \| Y_{\alpha} \| , \quad U_{\alpha} = Y_{\alpha} / R_{\alpha} .
$$

If the spatial median of Y α is zero, set

$$
A_{\alpha} = E \left [ \frac{I - U_{\alpha} U_{\alpha}^{T}}{R_{\alpha}} \right ] , \quad S_{\alpha} = V a r ( U_{\alpha} ) .
$$

Theorem 6 (Scale-dependent covariance) . Under the centered growingK regime of Theorem 4, if $W_b$ ⇝ W ∼ N (0 , Γ) , the spatial median of Y α = $H_{1}$ / 2 α W is zero, and $A_\alpha$ is nonsingular, then

$$
V_{\alpha} = H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} A_{\alpha}^{- 1} S_{\alpha} A_{\alpha}^{- T} H_{\alpha}^{- 1 / 2} .
$$

The formula shows exactly how α enters inference. The scale matrix first transforms the node error distribution from W to Y α = $H_{1}$ / 2 α W . The geometric median is most efficient when this transformed distribution is close to spherical. The final factor H − 1 / 2 $\alpha_c$onverts the covariance back to the original mean-subspace tangent coordinates.

For PCA, the covariance Γ decomposes as

$$
\Gamma = \begin{pmatrix} \Gamma_{\mu \mu} & \Gamma_{\mu \mathcal{U}} \\ \Gamma_{\mathcal{U} \mu} & \Gamma_{\mathcal{U} \mathcal{U}} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The block Γ µµ describes mean-estimation uncertainty; Γ UU describes eigenspace-estimation uncertainty; and the cross-blocks describe dependence between the two. Since Γ UU contains eigengap denominators through (15), weak eigengaps can strongly affect V α .

### 5.2 Whitening and block-noise calibration

A simple but useful case occurs when the mean and subspace blocks are approximately isotropic and weakly correlated:

$$
\Gamma \approx \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{\mu}^{2} I_{p} & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_{\mathcal{U}}^{2} I_{r ( p - r )} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Then the transformed covariance $H_{1}$ / 2 α Γ $H_{1}$ / 2 α is spherical when

$$
\alpha \sigma_{\mu}^{2} = ( 2 - \alpha ) \sigma_{\mathcal{U}}^{2} ,
$$

which gives

$$
\alpha_{\text{white}} = 2 \frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mu}^{2} + \sigma_{\mathcal{U}}^{2}} .
$$

More generally, define average block variances

$$
\tau_{\mu} = \frac{1}{p} \, \text{tr} ( \Gamma_{\mu \mu} ) , \quad \tau_{\mathcal{U}} = \frac{1}{r ( p - r )} \, \text{tr} ( \Gamma_{\mathcal{U} \mathcal{U}} ) .
$$

The block-noise calibration rule is

$$
\alpha_{b l o c k} = 2 \frac{\tau_{U}}{\tau_{\mu} + \tau_{U}} .
$$

This rule calibrates the metric to the uncertainty of the node estimators, not to raw data dispersion. In PCA terms, if the subspace estimator is noisy, then τ U is large and α block moves toward two, thereby downweighting the Grassmann term 2 − α . If the mean estimator is noisy, α block moves toward zero, downweighting the Euclidean term.

Proposition 7 (Efficiency under perfect whitening) . If Y α ∼ N (0 ,$\sigma_2$ I d ) , with d > 1 , then

$$
A_{\alpha}^{- 1} S_{\alpha} A_{\alpha}^{- T} = c_{d} \sigma^{2} I_{d} , \quad c_{d} = \frac{2 d \, \Gamma ( d / 2 )^{2}}{( d - 1 )^{2} \Gamma ( ( d - 1 ) / 2 )^{2}} .
$$

Moreover, c d → 1 as d → ∞ .

This proposition gives the efficiency intuition behind block-noise calibration. A spatial median is generally less efficient than a mean under exact Gaussianity, but when the transformed node errors are spherical the loss is summarized by a scalar constant that approaches one in high dimension. Calibration can therefore recover much of the efficiency while retaining median-of-means robustness to corrupted nodes.

## 6 Concentration, robustness, and resampling

The asymptotic results show how scale affects limiting inference. Median-of-means, however, is valued because it offers finite-sample robustness to corrupted nodes. This section gives deterministic and probabilistic guarantees that make the role of scale explicit.

### 6.1 Good-node concentration

We revisit the geometric core of MoM robustness, closely related to the majority-ball arguments used in robust mean estimation, that is deterministic and holds in any metric space. (Minsker; 2015; Lugosi and Mendelson; 2019).

Theorem 11 (Deterministic good-node lemma) . Let $z_{1}$ ,...,z K ∈ M p , and let

$$
\widetilde{z}_{\alpha} \in \arg \min_{z \in \mathcal{M}_{p}} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} d_{\alpha} ( z , z_{k} ) .
$$

Suppose at least (1 / 2 + γ ) K of the points satisfy d α ( z k ,$\theta_0$ ) ≤ r , where γ ∈ (0 , 1 / 2] . Then

$$
d_{\alpha} ( \widetilde{z}_{\alpha} , \theta_{0} ) \leq \left ( 1 + \frac{1}{2 \gamma} \right ) r .
$$

Combining this lemma with a node-level second-moment bound gives the usual MoM exponential tail form, now with the scaled product metric.

Theorem 12 (Scaled high-probability MoM bound) . Assume that for every node

$$
E d_{\alpha} ( \widehat{\theta}_{k} , \theta_{0} )^{2} \leq \frac{\alpha v_{\mu} + ( 2 - \alpha ) v_{\mathcal{U}}}{b}
$$

for constants v µ ,v U > 0 . Then there exist universal constants $c_{1}$ ,$c_{2}$ > 0 such that

$$
P \left ( d_{\alpha} ( \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha} , \theta_{0} ) > c_{1} \sqrt{\frac{\alpha v_{\mu} + ( 2 - \alpha ) v_{\mathcal{U}}}{b}} \right ) \leq \exp ( - c_{2} K ) .
$$

The theorem gives a bound in the scaled product metric, which leads to the following corollary that translates into the mean and the principal subspace.

Corollary 13 (Factorwise deviation tradeoff) . Under the conditions of Theorem 12, with probability at least 1 − exp( − $c_{2}$ K ) ,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\| \widetilde{\mu}_{n , \alpha} - \mu_{0} \| & \leq \frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \sqrt{\frac{\alpha v_{\mu} + ( 2 - \alpha ) v_{U}}{b}} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
d_{\text{Gr}} ( \widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{n , \alpha} , \mathcal{U}_{0} ) \leq \frac{c_{1}}{\sqrt{2 - \alpha}} \sqrt{\frac{\alpha v_{\mu} + ( 2 - \alpha ) v_{\mathcal{U}}}{b}} .
$$

This corollary is the finite-sample analogue of the covariance tradeoff. Moving α toward two may reduce the scaled effect of subspace noise, but it also weakens the direct subspace guarantee because the factor (2 − α ) − 1 / 2 grows. Moving α toward zero has the analogous effect on the mean.

### 6.2 Bad nodes and factorwise influence

The median aggregation tolerates arbitrary corrupted nodes as long as fewer than half the nodes are bad. This breakdown threshold does not depend on α . The direction and magnitude of factorwise influence do depend on α . In tangent coordinates, define the node score

$$
\psi_{\alpha} ( w ) = \frac{w}{\| w \|_{H_{\alpha}}} , \quad w = ( w_{\mu} , w_{\mathcal{U}} ) \neq 0 .
$$


Theorem 14 (Bad-node robustness and factorwise influence) . If fewer than half of the node estimators are arbitrary and the remaining nodes are contained in a bounded d α -ball around $\theta_0$ , then the scaled median-of-means estimator remains bounded. Moreover,

$$
\sup_{w \neq 0} \| \psi_{\alpha , \mu} ( w ) \| = \alpha^{- 1 / 2} ,
$$


and This result is practically important for our proposal. A node can be corrupted primarily in the mean while its subspace estimate remains plausible, or vice versa. The scale determines how visible such corruption is to the radial denominator in the median score.

$$
\sup_{w \neq 0} \| \psi_{\alpha , \mathcal{U}} ( w ) \| = ( 2 - \alpha )^{- 1 / 2} .
$$


Thus the breakdown fraction is scale-invariant, but the mean and subspace influence constants are scale-dependent.

### 6.3 Node bootstrap

For inference, one may either estimate V α by plug-in formulas or resample the node estimators. In this paper, we consider the node bootstrap, which is especially natural in distributed settings because it does not require resampling raw observations.

Let θ ∗ 1 ,..., θ ∗ K be sampled with replacement from θ 1 ,..., θ K , and define θ ∗ n,α by applying the same scaled median aggregation to the bootstrap node estimates.

Theorem 15 (Node bootstrap validity) . Under the centered growingK conditions of Theorem 4, conditionally on the observed nodes,

$$
\sqrt{n} \log_{\widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha}} \widetilde{\theta}_{n , \alpha}^{*} \sim \mathcal{N} ( 0 , V_{\alpha} )
$$

in probability. If α is estimated and the negligibility condition of Theorem 10 is not invoked, then the bootstrap procedure should recompute α ∗ inside each bootstrap replicate.

## 7 Experiments

### 7.1 Eigengap-driven subspace uncertainty

The first simulation examines whether the proposed scale calibration responds to the main statistical difficulty in PCA: instability of the leading eigenspace when the eigengap is small. We generated data from a spiked covariance model in $R^p$ with p = 200 and target rank r = 5. The sample size was n = 40000, split into K = 80 equal-sized nodes, and results were averaged over 60 Monte Carlo replications. The eigengap $\Delta =$ λ r − λ r +1 was varied over $\Delta$ ∈ { 0 . 1 , 0 . 25 , 0 . 5 , 1 , 2 , 4 } . For each node, we computed the node mean and leading $r$ -dimensional principal subspace. We compared fullsample PCA, random subset PCA using one node-sized subsample, projector-averaged distributed PCA, fixed-scale MoM PCA with α = 1, and the proposed robust scale-calibrated MoM PCA.

This experiment is designed to isolate the role of the eigengap. The influence expansion shows that the Grassmann component contains eigengap-weighted covariance perturbations. Thus, as $\Delta$ decreases, the node subspace estimates become more variable even when the node mean estimates remain stable. A useful scale calibration rule should detect this imbalance from node-level errors and downweight the Grassmann component when the subspace is weakly separated.

Figure 1 summarizes the estimation performance. The random subset baseline is unstable across all eigengaps, especially in the subspace coordinate. At $\Delta =$ 1, for example, the average subspace error of the random subset estimator is about 1 . 98, compared with 0 . 31 for the MoM estimators and 0 . 28 for projector averaging. Full-sample PCA is the clean-data reference and is, as expected, the most accurate estimator. Projector averaging is also efficient in this clean Gaussian setting. The MoM estimators pay a modest clean-efficiency cost relative to projector averaging, but remain far more stable than using a single random subset. This is the expected price of robust aggregation.

### 7.2 Factor-specific bad nodes and perturbation severity

The second simulation studies robustness when a minority of nodes are corrupted. We used the same spiked covariance model as in the previous experiment, with p = 200, r = 5, n = 40000, K = 80, and eigengap $\Delta =$ 1. Results were averaged over 50 Monte Carlo replications. We considered two types of bad nodes. In the mean-only contamination setting, the node mean was shifted while the node subspace was left unchanged. In the subspace-only contamination setting, the node subspace was perturbed while the node mean was left unchanged. We compared projector averaging, random subset PCA, fixed-scale MoM PCA with α ∈ { 0 . 25 , 1 , 1 . 75 } , and the robust scale-calibrated MoM estimator.

According to the factorwise robustness theory, the deterministic breakdown threshold of the median aggregation is governed by the fraction of bad nodes and is not changed by α . However, the factorwise influence constants depend on scale:

$$
\| \psi_{\alpha , \mu} \| \leq \alpha^{- 1 / 2} , \quad | | \psi_{\alpha , \mathcal{U}} \| \leq ( 2 - \alpha )^{- 1 / 2} .
$$

Thus, large α protects the mean coordinate by making mean deviations more visible in the product distance, whereas small α protects the subspace coordinate by making subspace deviations more visible. The simulation therefore asks not only whether MoM is robust, but also which factor is protected by a given scale.

## 8 Conclusion

This paper studied robust distributed PCA through the geometry of $R^p \times \mathrm{Gr}(r,p)$. The main message is that median-of-means aggregation of node PCA estimates is not only a robustness device, but also an inferential design problem. A node estimate contains a Euclidean mean and a Grassmann principal subspace, and the relative scale assigned to these components determines how node-level disagreement is measured. Consequently, scale affects covariance, finite-block bias, concentration, and factorwise robustness.

The theory explains why scale calibration is different in the MoM setting than in ordinary product medians. For ordinary product medians, changing factor weights can change the population center. In the present problem, the aggregated objects are node estimators, so scale acts on the node-error distribution. Since the mean influence is X − $\mu_0$ and the subspace influence is an eigengapweighted covariance perturbation, the relevant scale is a property of node-level uncertainty rather than raw measurement units.

The empirical results support this interpretation. The calibrated scale tracks eigengap-driven changes in subspace variability, and the bad-node experiments confirm the predicted factorwise tradeoff: large α protects the mean, whereas small α protects the subspace. The 10x mouse brain analysis shows that the method is feasible at large scale and that the selected scale changes with PCA rank. The method is therefore best viewed as a robust distributed alternative for heterogeneous, heavy-tailed, or partially corrupted settings, not as a replacement for standard PCA in clean centralized data.

Several directions remain open. The present theory is fixed-dimensional, while many PCA applications involve growing p , growing r , or structural constraints such as sparsity. Robust node-level covariance estimators could be incorporated when individual nodes are themselves contaminated. The joint design of the number of nodes K , block size b , and scale $\alpha$ also merits further study. More broadly, the same principle applies to any distributed procedure that produces heterogeneous product-valued summaries: the final robust aggregation should calibrate scale from node-level uncertainty rather than impose it externally.

### A.1 Proof of Theorem 1

The expansion of the mean is immediate:

$$
\sqrt{b} ( \widehat{\mu}_{k} - \mu_{0} ) = b^{- 1 / 2} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} ( X_{i} - \mu_{0} ) .
$$

For the covariance, replacing $\mu_0$ by $\mu_k$ changes the covariance estimator by a term

$$
( \widehat{\mu}_{k} - \mu_{0} ) ( \widehat{\mu}_{k} - \mu_{0} )^{T} ,
$$

which is O p ( b − 1 ) under the moment assumption and is therefore negligible after multiplication by √ b . Thus

$$
\sqrt{b} ( \widehat{\Sigma}_{k} - \Sigma ) = b^{- 1 / 2} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} \{( X_{i} - \mu_{0} ) ( X_{i} - \mu_{0} )^{T} - \Sigma \} + o_{p} ( 1 ) .
$$

Let $P_{0}$ = U r U T r be the spectral projector onto $U_0$ , and let P k be the projector onto U k . By the differentiability of spectral projectors under the eigengap condition,

$$
\widehat{P}_{k} - P_{0} = \mathcal{L}_{\Sigma} ( \widehat{\Sigma}_{k} - \Sigma ) + O_{p} ( \| \widehat{\Sigma}_{k} - \Sigma \|^{2} ) ,
$$

where

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma} ( E ) = \sum_{j = 1}^{r} \sum_{a = 1}^{p - r} \frac{u_{r + a}^{T} E u_{j}}{\lambda_{j} - \lambda_{r + a}} ( u_{r + a} u_{j}^{T} + u_{j} u_{r + a}^{T} ) .
$$

The moment assumption implies ∥ $\Sigma_k$ − Σ ∥ = O p ( b − 1 / 2 ), so the quadratic remainder is o p ( b − 1 / 2 ). In the Grassmann normal chart at $U_0$ , the tangent coordinate matrix for U k is, to first order, the off-diagonal block

$$
U_{\perp}^{T} \mathcal{L}_{\Sigma} ( \widehat{\Sigma}_{k} - \Sigma ) U_{r} .
$$

Substituting the covariance expansion yields

$$
\sqrt{b} \log_{U_{0}} ( \widehat{U}_{k} ) = b^{- 1 / 2} \sum_{i \in B_{k}} B ( X_{i} ) + o_{p} ( 1 ) ,
$$

with entries defined in (15). Combining the mean and subspace components gives (16). The multivariate central limit theorem applies because $E\|X-\mu_0\|^{4+\delta}<\infty$ implies $\zeta(X)$ has finite second moment, so the block averages are asymptotically Gaussian.

$$
\sqrt{b} \, Q_{K , \alpha} ( \exp_{\theta_{0}} ( v / \sqrt{b} ) ) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k = 1}^{K} \| v - W_{k , b} \|_{H_{\alpha}} + \Delta_{K , b , \alpha} ( v ) ,

where sup v ∈ C,α ∈ I ε | $\Delta$ K,b,α ( v ) | = o p (1) for fixed K on every compact set C . The objectives are convex in the tangent approximation and locally coercive under Assumption (A2). ## C Additional real-data analyses

This section reports two additional real-data analyses. The main paper focuses on the 10x mouse brain data because it is a large single-cell RNA-seq example where PCA is a standard preprocessing and visualization tool. Here we use two supplementary datasets to examine whether the same mean– subspace aggregation behavior appears in different settings. The first is a human single-cell dataset from Tabula Sapiens (The Tabula Sapiens Consortium et al.; 2022), which stays close to the singlecell motivation but introduces a different biological context. The second is a climate EOF/PCA example based on NOAA OISST sea-surface temperature fields (Reynolds et al.; 2002; Huang et al.; 2021), which tests the method outside genomics.

The purpose of these analyses is not to claim that one method is uniformly best in all real-data settings. Rather, the goal is to examine whether the empirical patterns from the main paper persist: random-subset PCA is unstable, distributed aggregation improves stability, fixed-scale MoM and scale-calibrated MoM produce competitive robust summaries, and the selected scale reflects nodelevel mean–subspace uncertainty. As in the main real-data example, full-data PCA is treated as a centralized reference, not as ground truth.

### C.1 Tabula Sapiens blood cells

We first analyzed the blood component of the Tabula Sapiens human single-cell atlas. Tabula Sapiens is a multi-organ single-cell reference atlas with rich biological metadata, and the blood subset provides a useful supplementary example because it is large enough to require distributed processing while remaining computationally manageable. We selected p = 2000 highly variable genes from the log-normalized expression matrix and compared distributed PCA summaries to the fulldata PCA reference. The analysis used n = 50115 cells, K ∈ { 20 , 50 } nodes, and ranks r ∈ { 10 , 20 } . For each node, we computed the node mean and node principal subspace, then compared projector averaging, random subset PCA, fixed-scale MoM PCA with α = 1, and scale-calibrated MoM PCA. Results were averaged over 10 random shardings.

This is expected because it discards most nodes and uses only one node-sized subsample. Projector averaging is highly accurate for the mean, but its subspace error increases with the number of nodes. The MoM estimators have slightly larger mean errors than projector averaging, but their mean errors remain very small on the scale of the lognormalized data. In contrast, their subspace errors are competitive and often smaller than projector averaging, especially for r = 20.

### C.2 NOAA OISST sea-surface temperature EOF analysis

We next considered a climate example based on NOAA Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST). Empirical orthogonal function analysis is the climate-science analogue of PCA, so this example provides a non-genomic check of the proposed aggregation framework. The OISST product is a gridded sea-surface temperature record constructed from satellite and in-situ observations. We formed an anomaly matrix from a regional, coarsened OISST field and treated time points as observations. The analysis used n = 120 time points, p = 80 spatial coordinates, K = 5 temporal nodes, and rank r = 3. Because the data are temporally dependent, this analysis should be interpreted as a cross-domain robustness and scalability illustration rather than a direct validation of the independent-sampling theory.

### C.3 Summary

The supplementary real-data analyses reinforce two practical lessons. First, random subset PCA is an unreliable baseline when the goal is to approximate a full-data mean–subspace summary; it often has much larger mean error or subspace error because it ignores most nodes. Second, robust MoM aggregation can improve subspace stability relative to simple distributed baselines, but the scale-calibrated version should be interpreted through the mean–subspace tradeoff. In Tabula Sapiens, the calibration identifies large node-level subspace dispersion and selects $\alpha_{\mathrm{rPCA}}$ close to 2. In OISST, the calibration selects a smaller value of α , improving mean stability while giving a modestly larger subspace error than fixed-scale MoM. Together with the main 10x mouse brain analysis, these examples show that scale calibration adapts to the empirical node-error geometry rather than enforcing a fixed universal preference for either the mean or the subspace component.
