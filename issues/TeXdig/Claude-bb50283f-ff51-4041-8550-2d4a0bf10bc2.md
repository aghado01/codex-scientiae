## What the witness actually does

It never touches PdfPig. It reads only the JSONL. That's the whole architectural point, and it's the answer to your language question hiding inside your other question.

**1. Admission → spine.** Admit `body`, `page-marker`, `float-caption`, `float-label`; drop `running-header` and `folio`. Each `float` record collapses to a single `U+FFFC` — a *hole* occupying exactly one code unit, so a non-text object still has coordinates. Join with `\n\n`. Two parallel strings fall out: the **spine** (recovered via `ConvertFrom-PdfProseUtf16Escape` of `textEscaped`) and **`$expected`** (the concatenated `textRender`). 196 blocks, 1 hole, 86,859 chars.

**2. Mint a master.** `TextMaster::new(id, rev 1, spine)` — identity, fingerprint, total atom topology. 86,859 chars = 86,859 atoms, so all BMP here.

**3. Collect claims from an inventory.** [macy-spine.jsonl](src/pdf-prose/inventories/macy-spine.jsonl) is six rules — guillemets, page-marker, figure-cue, float-hole, speaker-start — and the speaker rule is a literal alternation of *Pitts|Shannon|Teuber|McCulloch|Savage|Wiener|…*. ## Hosting compiled .NET from non-PowerShell runtimes

Your current vendoring is loose DLLs plus per-assembly `.deps.json` and **no `.runtimeconfig.json`**. `Add-Type -Path` works because pwsh 7 *is already a running .NET host* — it just loads into the existing context. Nothing else gets that for free.

| Host | In-process route | Reality |
|---|---|---|
| **C#/.NET** | `PackageReference` / `ProjectReference` | Non-problem. Full type fidelity. |
| **Python** | `pythonnet` (`import clr`) | Mature, hosts CoreCLR. But framework-dependent assemblies want a `runtimeconfig.json` you don't have — practical fix is to build a thin C# shim project, publish it, point pythonnet at *that* output dir. |
| **Python** | NativeAOT + `ctypes`/`cffi` | Write a `[UnmanagedCallersOnly]` shim, `PublishAot`, hand-roll the ABI: UTF-8 in, pointer+length out, explicit free. Clean at runtime, but you own every crossing. |
| **Node** | `edge-js` | Effectively unmaintained against modern .NET. Don't build on it. |
| **Node** | NativeAOT + `koffi` (or an N-API addon) | Same shim story as ctypes. `koffi` is the maintained FFI. |
| **Any** | Subprocess / stdio worker + JSONL | Boring, reliable, already your house pattern. |

The honest generalization: **there is no good in-process .NET story from Python or Node that doesn't route through either CoreCLR-hosting fragility or a C-ABI shim you write and maintain.** Both flatten rich types to bytes at the boundary. And look at what would have to cross:

Generic static methods taking delegates. That's already the ugliest code in the witness *from PowerShell*, which is a .NET host. Over FFI it isn't expressible — you'd have to redesign doccer's surface into a data-driven one purely to get it across. At which point your shim is a C# program, and you've written the C# converter anyway plus an FFI tax.

## The folio is not text that happens to be at the top

A folio's **signified** is an index over a document segment. Its **signifier** is three glyphs in a box. pdf-prose classifies it by the signifier — `Top > Height * 0.92`, `^\d+$` — and having classified it, files it as a `block` with `role: "folio"`, i.e. as *prose that happens to be short and high on the page*. It's not prose at all. It's an attribute of the page, and the page is the thing that got segmented.

That's the rendering-pathway error committed *inside* the transfer pathway: the renderer's job is to turn a document-level attribute into marks on a surface, and pdf-prose read the marks back as if the marks were the level.

And the code contradicts itself about this. The witness admits `page-marker` to the spine — all 36 `[n]` — and drops `folio`. Those are the same kind of thing at different provenance: `[n]` is the 2016 edition transcribing the *original* pagination inline, the folio is the 2016 edition's *own* pagination. Two segmentation markers over two different segmentations of the same text. One is treated as content-bearing and gets a claim in the inventory; the other is treated as trash. Neither is treated as what it is.

Same for running headers. 24 `running-header` blocks is 24 stampings of *one* section-level fact. The repetition is the signal and it's being emitted as if it were twenty-four pieces of text.

## The invariant, since that's what you asked for

**A mark's level is not its position's level, and repetition across segments is the evidence that recovers it.**

Something that recurs once per segment at a stable position is an attribute *of the segment*, not content *in* it. That's measurable — recurrence rate against segment count, positional variance — and it's genre-agnostic: it fires on folios, running heads, watermarks, letterheads, table headers repeated across a page break, slide numbers. No names, no `Fig\.`, no 0.92.

The value inference is a second, separate step and it's also invariant-shaped: among per-segment attributes, the one whose values form a monotone integer sequence is an **ordinal index over the segmentation**. That's what makes it *usable* rather than merely removable — it lets you detect the offset between printed folio and PDF page index, roman-numeral front matter, plates that break the sequence, missing leaves. The rendering pathway has no use for any of that. The transfer pathway needs all of it.

## What that does to the checkpoint

Two things follow that I think bear on the IR directly.

The IR has no `page` record. `document`, `section`, `block`, `float` — and `page` exists only as an integer field scattered across blocks. So the segmentation the folio *names* has no object to be an attribute of. The folio got demoted to a block partly because there was nowhere else to put it.

And the sharper one, for a checkpoint whose purpose is comparing lanes: **pagination exists in only one of your two lanes.** LaTeX source has no page numbers — pagination is output of TeX's page-breaking, and TeXdig by design refuses to execute. So folio, running head, page break, and column break are all things the PDF lane knows and the LaTeX lane structurally *cannot* know without running TeX. That's the largest asymmetry between the two lanes, and it's the mirror of the one you already have going the other way (LaTeX knows `\caption` is a caption; PDF has to abduct it).

Which means the IR has to distinguish *this lane knows there is none* from *this lane cannot know* — the knowability binary applied across lanes rather than within one — or every measurement at the checkpoint scores a structural blind spot as a disagreement.

**The clause is technically true but not for the reason it implies.** JSON Schema can't express foreign keys because it has no value binding or quantification over the instance — you cannot capture an edge's `source` and assert the existence of a sibling whose `id` equals it. That's not a *scope* limitation ("same store"), it's absent from the language even within a single document. `$ref` is schema reuse, not data reference; `contains` can't be parameterized by another item's value. Ajv's `$data` extension does exactly this, and using it would make the schemas Ajv-specific — which forfeits the cross-language neutrality that's the whole reason the schema is normative. So: don't.

**But the conclusion is a non-sequitur.** "Not expressible in JSON Schema" → "and is not checked here" doesn't follow. Existence checking over a JSONL store is one pass building a set of ids and one pass testing endpoints, and jsonl_engine already reads every line. The doc lets the tool's limit stand in for the world's.

**And the bigger miss is that "referential integrity" is two things, and only one of them is inexpressible.**

- *Referential **typing*** — does this id point at the right **class** of thing — is plain `pattern` validation against your id grammar. Fully standard, works in every validator, catches most of what people actually want foreign keys for.
- *Referential **existence*** — does the target exist — is the part that genuinely can't be schema'd.

And the primitive throws the first one away: `"nodeId": {"type": "string", "minLength": 1}`. Your entire `{class}:{locator}` grammar — `zone:`, `walk:`, `ent:`, `def:`, `occ:`, `bind:`, `inv:` — the verbatim join key that T2 made canonical, reduced to "a nonempty string" at the boundary. That's expressive power sitting unused, and it undercuts the fatalism more directly than the arity point does.

**Which is where arity comes in.** A binary `{source, target, relation}` edge is so structurally thin that there's almost nothing for a schema to say about it — two opaque strings and a name. That thinness is probably why "integrity is inexpressible" reads as though it were the whole story. A typed n-ary hyperedge gives the schema real work:

```json
{"type":"edge","relation":"float-unit",
 "members":[{"role":"figure","node":"zone:0012"},
            {"role":"caption","node":"zone:0013"},
            {"role":"anchor","node":"walk:0441"}]}
```

Now `oneOf` on `relation` can constrain member arity per relation, `role` becomes an enum, and each role's `node` gets a `pattern` pinning it to its id class — a `caption` member must be a `zone:`, an `anchor` must be a `walk:`. All standard, all checkable line-by-line, none of it possible with two bare strings.

So going n-ary doesn't cost you schema coverage. It's the move that gives the schema something to validate.

# isn't there such a thing as 'hyperschemas'?

Yes, but it's a false cognate — the "hyper" is **hypertext**, not hypergraph. JSON Hyper-Schema is a hypermedia vocabulary: it describes hyperlinks and how a client should construct HTTP requests from a JSON document. Nothing to do with n-ary relations.

What it actually gives you is `links` — an array of Link Description Objects, each with `rel`, an `href` URI Template that interpolates values out of the instance, plus `targetSchema` / `submissionSchema` describing what's at the other end. It's HATEOAS for JSON.

Two reasons it doesn't help here:

**It's annotation, not assertion.** Hyper-Schema keywords produce annotations; they never fail validation. A `links` entry pointing at a nonexistent resource is not a schema violation — the vocabulary has no opinion about whether the target exists, because resolving it is the client's job at request time. So it doesn't close the gap you're poking at.

**It's effectively abandoned.** It ran through draft-07 and 2019-09, was not carried forward into 2020-12, and the maintainers put it on indefinite hold pending a rethink. Tooling support outside a couple of JS libraries is nil. Building the neutral cross-lane contract on it would be building on a dead branch.

There is one idea worth stealing without the spec, though: `targetSchema` expresses *"this field references something conforming to schema S"* declaratively. That's a cleaner statement of intent than a regex, and it's the same thing the `pattern`-on-id-grammar move buys you practically. You could carry it as a property of your relation vocabulary — each relation declaring, per role, which node class it admits — and then mechanically emit both the `pattern` constraints and the tier-3 existence check from that one declaration. One authored fact, two enforcement surfaces.

**If you want genuinely declarative referential integrity, the technology that does it is SHACL** — W3C, designed for validating graph shapes, with real `sh:class` targeting, `sh:minCount`/`sh:maxCount` cardinality, and property paths. It would give you integrity and arity constraints properly.

The catch is exactly your sore spot: SHACL validates RDF, and RDF is triples. Which leaves the boring answer standing: the existence check is a set build and a membership test, in the one component that already reads every line.

# what about something like parquet

Parquet is genuinely stronger than JSON Schema on some axes and worse on the one your design leans hardest on.

**Where it wins.** The schema is *in the file* rather than in a separate document you hope someone applied. Physical plus logical types, `required`/`optional`/`repeated` as structural Dremel levels rather than convention, and nested repeated groups natively — a `members: list<struct<role, node>>` is a first-class Parquet schema, not a JSON blob in a column. That's a real fit for the n-ary hyperedge shape.

**Where it breaks against your design: no sum types.** Parquet has no usable union. Your entire contract tier is discriminated unions — `node | edge`, `WalkNode` = `section | paragraph | anchor`. JSON Schema's `oneOf` with a `const` discriminator says *"when kind is section, title and level are required."* Parquet cannot express that. Your options are one wide table with everything nullable plus a `kind` column (strictly weaker than what you have now), one file per variant (loses the single ordered walk), or a struct-per-variant with exactly-one-populated by convention (unenforced). All three are regressions.

**And it costs you things you already have.** Parquet bytes are not stable across writer versions, compression codecs, row-group sizing, dictionary encoding, or statistics settings — which collides directly with your two-run byte-determinism gate, a live acceptance condition. You'd also lose grep, `tail`, and git diffs on stores. Append is buffered row groups rather than a line at a time.

**Node is the weak lane for it.** parquet-dotnet and pyarrow are solid; the JS/TS story is materially thinner. That penalizes TeXdig asymmetrically, which is the opposite of what a neutral convergence format should do.

**And it still doesn't do referential integrity.** Same gap as JSON Schema — it's a file format, not a database. No foreign keys.

**Where it actually fits.** Your convergence IR serves two different workloads and they want different things:

- *decode to markdown* — streaming, ordered, per-document. JSONL is correct.
- *compare the lanes* — analytical, join-and-aggregate across a corpus. That's precisely the columnar workload, and it's your own doccer thesis: the tree as a query/view over sorted columns.

So the split I'd make is JSONL normative — diffable, deterministic, streamable, union-capable — with Parquet or in-memory Arrow as a **derived, regenerable projection** built for the measurement pass. That lands it in your `artifacts/` tier rather than the contract tier, and nothing about it needs to be authoritative.

If the itch is specifically *"C# and Node must agree on a typed record,"* Arrow is the sharper tool than Parquet — real implementations in both, schema stronger than JSON Schema, zero-copy IPC. Same union gap, though. It doesn't rescue the discriminator.
